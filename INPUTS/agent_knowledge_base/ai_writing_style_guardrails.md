# AI Writing Style Guardrails

## Purpose

Use this file when drafting or editing public-facing articles, LinkedIn posts, decks, cover notes, employer-facing notes, or PM_pitch materials.

The goal is to prevent AI-assisted writing from sounding generic, over-polished, formulaic, artificially dramatic, or detached from the writer's own judgment. This guidance complements `mykola_writing_style.md`; it does not replace it.

Use this file especially before finalizing:
- company articles
- thought-leadership drafts
- pitch decks
- LinkedIn posts
- recruiter or hiring-manager notes
- external-safe research summaries
- public recommendations based on evidence and hypotheses

## Core Principle

AI can help with research, structure, challenge, compression, source mapping, and editing.

It should not replace the writer's point of view.

The problem is not that a sentence contains one suspicious word or punctuation mark. The problem is a cluster of symptoms:

- generic claims
- smooth but weak reasoning
- over-neat structure
- fashionable vocabulary
- missing lived judgment
- examples that sound specific but are not
- recommendations that do not follow from evidence
- wording that could apply to any company

For Mykola's materials, the final writing should feel:

- specific
- grounded in evidence
- opinionated where the evidence supports it
- direct
- practical
- human
- slightly understated
- willing to say "we do not know" where evidence is missing

It should not feel:

- auto-generated
- over-symmetrical
- artificially energetic
- padded with fashionable business language
- written to impress rather than explain
- so polished that it no longer sounds like a person thinking

## Detection Caveat

Do not use this file as a mechanical AI detector.

Rules:

- One sign does not prove AI writing.
- A cluster of signs should trigger review.
- Human corporate writing often has the same weaknesses.
- AI-polished human writing can be wrongly flagged as AI-generated.
- The goal is not to "hide AI". The goal is to restore judgment, specificity, evidence, and voice.

The best test:

```text
Could the author explain why this sentence, example, structure, and claim are here?
```

If not, rewrite.

## What To Use AI For

Use AI for:

- summarizing source material
- finding gaps in the argument
- challenging unsupported claims
- building evidence matrices
- checking whether claims are too broad
- suggesting alternative structures
- compressing verbose drafts
- identifying repetition
- checking whether recommendations follow from evidence
- detecting over-polished or generic prose
- creating first-pass slide storyboards for human editing

Use AI cautiously for:

- full article drafting
- final wording
- personal stories
- hiring-manager outreach
- LinkedIn posts
- any material that needs to sound like Mykola

Avoid using AI for:

- inventing personal experience
- inventing company-specific pain points
- writing "authentic" emotion
- creating final thought leadership without human thesis control
- filling evidence gaps with plausible wording
- polishing until the writer's friction disappears

## Common AI-Sounding Patterns To Remove

### 1. Lazy Em Dash Usage

Avoid using em dashes as the default way to create rhythm, drama, or contrast.

Rule:

- Use commas, periods, colons, or parentheses first.
- Use a dash only when it is genuinely the best punctuation choice.
- If there are several dashes in a short piece, rewrite.
- Do not replace every dash with a hyphen just to hide the pattern. Fix the sentence.

Preferred:

```text
Volvo's challenge is not only electrification. It is the operating model required to make electrification useful for customers.
```

Avoid:

```text
Volvo's challenge is not only electrification - it is the operating model required to make electrification useful for customers.
```

### 2. Formulaic Contrast Structures

AI often creates too-clean contrast even when the idea needs a more natural sentence.

High-risk patterns:

- "It is not just X. It is Y."
- "It is not X, but Y."
- "This is not about X. It is about Y."
- "X rather than Y."
- "No X. No Y. Just Z."
- "From X to Y."
- "Far more than X..."

These patterns are not banned, but repeated use makes writing feel generated. Use once only if it genuinely sharpens the thesis.

Preferred:

```text
The visible story is electric trucks. The more interesting operating question is how Volvo connects asset data, service data, finance, and customer economics into decisions that hold up through the cycle.
```

Avoid:

```text
It is not just about electric trucks. It is about unlocking the future of connected mobility.
```

### 3. Rule-Of-Three Addiction

Groups of three are useful. AI overuses them because they sound complete.

Watch for:

- three adjectives in a row
- three verbs in a row
- three-part lists in every paragraph
- three-card slide layouts repeated too often
- "identify, evaluate, recommend" style phrasing
- list items with identical grammatical structure

Rule:

- Use the real number of points.
- If there are two, use two.
- If there are four, use four.
- If one point matters much more, give it more space.

Human writing is allowed to be lumpy.

### 4. Forced Drama Or Fake Intimacy

Avoid phrases that create artificial tension or pretend to reveal something.

High-risk patterns:

- "But here's the thing"
- "Here's the truth"
- "The uncomfortable truth"
- "What nobody is saying"
- "The result?"
- "And honestly?"
- "Hot take"
- "The part most people miss"
- "Let that sink in"
- "Are you ready to go deeper?"
- "You're not imagining it"

Preferred:

```text
The more useful question is whether Volvo can turn product, service, finance, and fleet data into a stronger decision system.
```

Avoid:

```text
But here's the thing: Volvo's real transformation is not what everyone thinks.
```

### 5. Buzzword Inflation

Avoid fashionable words when simpler words are more precise.

High-risk words and phrases:

- unlock
- elevate
- empower
- delve
- foster
- leverage
- utilize
- seamless
- robust
- dynamic
- transformative
- landscape
- ecosystem, when used vaguely
- tapestry
- navigate
- paradigm
- synergy
- game-changing
- next-level
- holistic
- nuanced, when used as filler
- pivotal
- crucial
- at scale, when used vaguely

Newer 2026 watchlist:

- quietly
- shift
- matters
- shape
- land
- actually
- real, when used as an intensifier
- earn, when attached to abstract nouns
- the work
- hold, when used metaphorically
- pull, when used as vague force
- compound
- signal, when it replaces a concrete event
- built different
- amid
- gap
- toolkit
- move
- bucket

Nuance:

- Some words, such as "important", "grounded", "complexity", "transformation", "platform", "governance", and "signal", can be valid in PM_pitch work.
- Keep them only when they carry specific meaning.
- Replace vague abstractions with concrete business mechanisms.

Preferred:

```text
The value comes from faster service decisions, better parts planning, and clearer customer readiness evidence.
```

Avoid:

```text
The value comes from unlocking a dynamic and seamless transformation journey.
```

### 6. Cliche Openings

Avoid opening with generic business context.

High-risk openings:

- "In today's fast-paced digital world..."
- "In the dynamic landscape of..."
- "As the world continues to evolve..."
- "Companies today face unprecedented change..."
- "In an era of rapid transformation..."
- "In an increasingly complex world..."
- "When it comes to..."
- "At the heart of..."

Preferred:

```text
Volvo Group is a useful case because it sits at the intersection of industrial cyclicality, service economics, and technology change.
```

Avoid:

```text
In today's rapidly evolving mobility landscape, Volvo Group faces both challenges and opportunities.
```

### 7. Over-Neat Lists

Lists are useful when the reader needs comparison, sequence, or decisions.

Avoid lists when they are used only to make weak writing look structured.

Before using bullets, ask:

- Does this list compare distinct items?
- Does the reader need to scan this?
- Would this work better as one direct paragraph?
- Are the bullets parallel because the ideas are parallel, or because AI made them symmetrical?
- Are there exactly three or five items only because the structure feels tidy?

For articles:

- Use fewer bullets.
- Prefer paragraphs with a clear point of view.
- Use bold sparingly to help scanning.

For decks:

- Use one claim per slide.
- Use short labels, not paragraph bullets.
- Put nuance in speaker notes or supporting material.

### 8. Generic Specificity

AI often gives examples that sound concrete but are still generic.

High-risk pattern:

```text
A global manufacturer may struggle to connect finance, operations, and service data.
```

Better:

```text
For Volvo Group, the harder question is how truck, construction equipment, dealer, service, finance, and customer lifecycle data connect into decisions about uptime, parts, financing, and electrification readiness.
```

Rule:

- Name the company-specific mechanism.
- Name the business capability.
- Name the data object or decision.
- Name what changes if the recommendation works.

### 9. Superficial Importance Claims

Avoid puffing up significance without showing the mechanism.

High-risk patterns:

- "plays a key role"
- "serves as a testament"
- "marks a pivotal moment"
- "represents a significant shift"
- "underscores the importance"
- "reflects broader trends"
- "sets the stage for"
- "highlights the need"

Preferred:

```text
Service sales matter because they can stabilize earnings when new-equipment demand weakens.
```

Avoid:

```text
Volvo's service business underscores the importance of transformation in a changing mobility landscape.
```

### 10. Hedge Density

Evidence-based writing needs caveats. AI adds too many.

Watch for:

- may
- might
- could
- potentially
- broadly
- often
- generally
- typically
- in some cases
- it is possible that

Rule:

- If the evidence supports the claim, state it.
- If the evidence is incomplete, mark the assumption once.
- Do not hedge every sentence.

Preferred:

```text
This is a hypothesis. Public evidence supports the direction, but not the internal architecture detail.
```

Avoid:

```text
This could potentially suggest that Volvo may possibly benefit from a more robust and holistic approach.
```

### 11. Conceptual Incoherence

This is more important than any single word.

AI can combine words that often appear together but do not fit conceptually.

Watch for:

- mixed metaphors
- "toolkits" that buy things
- "pillars" that both support and sit above something
- "journeys" that are not sequences
- "ecosystems" that are just a list of systems
- "signals" that do not signal anything specific
- "platforms" used for every system
- "decision engines" with no decision owner

Rule:

```text
If the metaphor cannot be drawn, explained, or defended, remove it.
```

For PM_pitch:

- Prefer business mechanisms over metaphor.
- Prefer capability maps over vague transformation language.
- Prefer decision paths over "journeys".

### 12. Canned Perfection

AI writing often feels too smooth: clean grammar, even paragraphs, polite transitions, no friction, no wrong turns, no irritation, no surprise.

Do not add fake mistakes. Instead, restore the thinking process:

- show the trade-off
- admit the weak source
- say what you are excluding
- keep one sharp judgment
- include one company-specific detail
- let some sections be shorter than others
- challenge the premise if needed

The aim is not messiness. The aim is evidence of judgment.

## PM_pitch Article Rules

Before drafting an article:

1. Write the thesis in one sentence.
2. Write the "obvious story vs real story" contrast.
3. Select three or four obstacles only.
4. Build an evidence matrix.
5. Decide what not to cover.
6. Define the diagram logic before creating visuals.

During drafting:

- Start with the company-specific tension, not broad industry context.
- Separate public fact from interpretation.
- Use evidence links for claims that matter.
- Make assumptions visible.
- Avoid claiming internal knowledge.
- Keep practical recommendations attached to each obstacle.
- Use confident wording when evidence supports it.
- Use caveats only where needed.
- Let paragraphs vary in length.
- Avoid symmetrical sections unless the argument truly needs symmetry.

During editing:

- Remove generic AI transitions.
- Cut abstract adjectives.
- Replace inflated phrasing with specific mechanisms.
- Check whether every paragraph advances the thesis.
- Check whether the article still sounds like Mykola.
- Read the draft aloud and mark any sentence that sounds like a LinkedIn template.

## PM_pitch Deck Rules

For slides:

- One slide, one job.
- One title, one claim.
- Keep visible text light.
- Avoid turning every slide into three cards or three bullets.
- Do not use "journey" language unless the slide literally shows a sequence.
- Avoid generic transformation diagrams unless they explain a specific company problem.
- Avoid repeating the same three-column structure.
- Avoid all headings in title case unless the chosen template requires it.
- Do not bold the first words of every bullet by default.

For speaker notes:

- Store source links and evidence notes.
- Distinguish evidenced claims from interpretation.
- Put uncertainty in notes if it would clutter the slide.
- Keep notes factual. Do not use notes to hide weak reasoning.

For recommendations:

- Tie each recommendation to a real business pressure.
- Show value logic, not only technology logic.
- Include governance and adoption implications.
- Name the decision owner or capability where possible.
- Avoid "AI can help" unless the required data, workflow, and control are named.

## Use / Do Not Use

### Use

- direct claims
- named evidence
- specific business mechanisms
- simple verbs
- one clear thesis
- uneven emphasis where warranted
- honest uncertainty
- concrete examples
- concise caveats
- practical recommendations
- source-to-claim mapping

### Do Not Use

- generic industry openings
- fake-reveal phrases
- repeated contrast reframes
- repetitive triplets
- abstract value claims
- over-polished LinkedIn cadence
- vague "transformation journey" language
- unsupported significance claims
- "AI can unlock..." sentences
- invented concept labels unless clearly defined
- rhetorical questions that are just transitions
- summaries that repeat what the reader just read

## Editing Checklist

Run this pass before finalizing external-facing material:

- Does the opening avoid generic industry setup?
- Is the thesis specific to the company?
- Are facts and hypotheses clearly separated?
- Are there unnecessary dashes?
- Are there fake-drama phrases?
- Are there fashionable AI buzzwords that can be replaced?
- Are there repeated "not X but Y" structures?
- Are there too many groups of three?
- Are bullets used because the content needs them?
- Does each paragraph or slide make a specific point?
- Are examples truly specific, or only specific-sounding?
- Is there conceptual coherence between the words?
- Is every recommendation tied to evidence, capability, or value?
- Are there too many hedges?
- Are sections suspiciously equal in length and rhythm?
- Could Mykola plausibly say this in a real conversation?
- Could Mykola defend the claim in an interview?

## Replacement Moves

If the writing sounds AI-generated:

- Shorten the sentence.
- Remove the dramatic transition.
- Replace the abstract noun with the business mechanism.
- Replace the slogan with an observation.
- Replace "transformation journey" with the actual sequence.
- Replace "unlock value" with the specific value driver.
- Replace "navigate complexity" with the concrete complexity.
- Replace "seamless" with the operating requirement.
- Replace "landscape" with the actual market, system, or operating context.
- Replace "this matters because" with the consequence.
- Replace "may potentially" with either "does" or "we do not know yet".
- Remove one item from a forced three-part list.
- Add the missing point of view.
- Add the source or remove the claim.
- Break symmetry where the argument is not symmetrical.

## PM_pitch Volvo Example

Weak:

```text
In today's rapidly evolving mobility landscape, Volvo Group must navigate a complex transformation journey to unlock seamless value at scale.
```

Better:

```text
Volvo Group's harder problem is connecting trucks, construction equipment, service events, dealers, finance, and customer operations into decisions that protect margin through the cycle.
```

Weak:

```text
This is not just about electrification. It is about empowering a dynamic ecosystem.
```

Better:

```text
Electric trucks create the visible story. The operating question is whether customers can prove route fit, charging readiness, service model, financing case, and uptime risk before they commit.
```

Weak:

```text
AI can unlock insights, streamline workflows, and foster collaboration.
```

Better:

```text
AI is useful only where Volvo can show the source data, decision owner, confidence level, and approval path. Service triage, warranty pattern detection, parts risk, and electrification-readiness briefs are more credible than a generic AI platform story.
```

## Relationship To Mykola Writing Style

`mykola_writing_style.md` defines the voice.

This file defines the anti-AI cleanup pass.

When the two conflict:

1. Preserve authenticity.
2. Preserve evidence accuracy.
3. Preserve directness.
4. Reduce polish before adding polish.

Final rule:

```text
If the writing sounds like it could be used for any company, sharpen the company-specific signal.
If it sounds like it was written to sound clever, make it useful instead.
If it sounds too smooth, check whether the thinking has been outsourced.
```
