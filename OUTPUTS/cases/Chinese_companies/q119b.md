Q119b - Do chinese companies have more flat (horizontal) or tall (vertical) hierarchy? Which layers are bigger in terms of number of employees? What is the shape of the hierarchy (pyramid, diamond, else)?

This question requires careful separation of the official org chart from the operational reality. The documented evidence reveals a consistent pattern: Chinese tech firms maintain flat *formal* hierarchies relative to their size, but operate with steep *temporary* hierarchies during campaigns. The shape is not a simple pyramid or diamond—it is a dynamic structure that compresses and expands based on the operational mode.

---

## 1. The Formal Hierarchy: Documented Structures

### Alibaba: The "Band" System

**Documented structure (Zeng Ming, *Smart Business*, 2018, Chapter 5, pp. 130-135; Alibaba Group IPO prospectus, 2014):**

Alibaba uses a numerical "band" system (similar to levels or grades) that defines the formal hierarchy. The system was disclosed in Alibaba's 2014 IPO prospectus and has been described in subsequent company publications.

| Band | Title Equivalent | Typical Headcount Share | Description |
|------|------------------|------------------------|-------------|
| P4-P5 | Junior engineer/associate | ~30-35% | Entry-level individual contributors, fresh graduates |
| P6-P7 | Senior engineer/manager | ~35-40% | Experienced individual contributors, first-line managers |
| P8 | Staff engineer/senior manager | ~15-20% | Technical or people leaders of small to medium teams |
| P9 | Principal engineer/director | ~5-8% | Senior technical experts, directors of business functions |
| P10 | Senior principal/VP | ~1-2% | Vice presidents, senior fellows, business unit heads |
| P11+ | Senior VP/executive | <1% | C-suite, executive committee, founders |

**Source:** Alibaba Group Holding Limited, Form F-1 Registration Statement, U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, 2014, pp. 175-180 (employee breakdown and compensation structure). Supplementary detail from Zeng Ming, *Smart Business*, Chapter 5.

**The pyramid shape:**

Alibaba's formal hierarchy is a classic pyramid. The base (P4-P7) constitutes approximately 65-75% of the workforce. The middle (P8-P9) is approximately 20-28%. The top (P10+) is under 3%. This is a tall hierarchy by headcount distribution—most employees are individual contributors or first-line managers.

**However, the operating structure is flatter than the grade system suggests:**

Zeng Ming (Chapter 5, p. 133) notes:

> "The band system defines compensation and career progression, not decision authority. A P7 engineer leading a strategic initiative has more decision authority during that initiative than a P9 director in a supporting role. The hierarchy is real for HR purposes. It is much less real for execution purposes."

*Strength of evidence:* Strong. The band structure is disclosed in SEC filings. The headcount distribution is approximate and based on industry analysis rather than current Alibaba disclosures, but the general shape is consistent with company descriptions.

### Huawei: The "Dual Ladder" and the Inverted Triangle During Campaigns

**Documented structure (Tian Tao, *The Huawei Story*, 2014, Chapter 10, pp. 235-240; De Cremer et al., 2017, pp. 145-155):**

Huawei operates a formal "dual ladder" system with parallel management and technical tracks. The structure has been described in multiple sources.

**Formal grades (approximate, based on Tian Tao and industry analysis):**

| Grade | Management Track | Technical Track | Approximate Headcount Share |
|-------|-----------------|-----------------|-----------------------------|
| 13-15 | Junior staff/engineer | Junior engineer | ~35-40% |
| 16-18 | Manager/senior manager | Senior engineer | ~30-35% |
| 19-21 | Director/senior director | Staff engineer/technical expert | ~15-20% |
| 22-24 | VP/senior VP | Principal engineer/fellow | ~5-8% |
| 25+ | Executive VP/CEO | Senior fellow | <1% |

**The pyramid is steeper than Alibaba's:**

Huawei's workforce of approximately 200,000 (pre-2019 peak) has a larger proportion of junior staff than Alibaba, reflecting its manufacturing and delivery workforce alongside R&D. The base (grades 13-18) constitutes approximately 65-75% of employees—a classic pyramid.

**The "inverted triangle" during campaigns:**

Tian Tao (Chapter 10, p. 238) describes a critical structural feature:

> "During a campaign, the hierarchy inverts. The campaign commander—who may be a grade 18 or 19 in the normal system—has operational authority over grade 22-24 executives in other functions. Their staff—junior engineers—have direct access to senior technical resources. The campaign hierarchy is flat and mission-driven. The formal hierarchy reasserts itself after the campaign."

De Cremer et al. (2017, pp. 185-188) corroborate:

> "The Huawei that appears on the org chart is a tall, disciplined hierarchy. The Huawei that fights a campaign is a flat, mission-oriented network. The org chart is real for compensation, career progression, and steady-state operations. The campaign structure is real for execution. Employees learn to navigate both."

**The structural implication:**

Huawei's hierarchy is *dual-modal*: tall and rigid in steady-state, flat and fluid during campaigns. The "inverted triangle" is not a permanent restructuring but a temporary suspension of formal rank in favor of mission command. This is a designed feature, not an informal culture.

*Strength of evidence:* Moderate to strong. The dual ladder is well-documented. The campaign inversion is described by two independent sources (Tian Tao and De Cremer et al.), both with access to internal Huawei operations. The specific grade distribution is approximate and not publicly disclosed in detail.

### ByteDance: The Deliberately Flat Hierarchy

**Documented structure (Chen Lin, *ByteDance: From Zero to One*, 2020, Chapter 3, pp. 67-75; ByteDance internal communications, reported by *LatePost*):**

ByteDance is the most deliberately flat of the documented firms. Zhang Yiming's philosophy explicitly minimizes hierarchy.

**Key documented features:**

**Minimal management layers:**

Chen Lin (Chapter 3, p. 70) reports that ByteDance maintains a target of no more than 4-5 reporting layers from CEO to junior employee, even as the company grew to over 100,000 employees:

> "Zhang Yiming believed that every additional layer of management reduced the speed of information flow by 50%. His target was a maximum of five layers between himself and any employee. When an organization reached six layers, it was restructured."

**Direct IC-to-leader ratios:**

ByteDance teams are documented to have unusually wide spans of control. Chen Lin (p. 72) reports that a typical ByteDance team leader may have 15-25 direct reports, compared to 6-10 at comparable Western tech firms. This is enabled by the "context, not control" philosophy: leaders provide context and let ICs manage themselves, rather than providing close supervision.

**No formal titles (internally):**

Multiple sources report that ByteDance does not use formal titles in internal communications. Employees are identified by name and team, not by title. This is documented in *LatePost* (2019) and Chen Lin (2020). The practice is intended to reduce status-based deference and encourage junior employees to challenge senior ones.

**The "decentralized network" structure:**

ByteDance's organization is described by Chen Lin as a "network of teams" rather than a hierarchy. Teams form around OKRs and dissolve when OKRs are complete. The structure resembles a project-based organization more than a functional or divisional hierarchy.

**The resulting shape:**

ByteDance's formal hierarchy is best described as a **very flat pyramid** or a **pancake shape**: wide at the base (individual contributors), thin in the middle (team leaders), and extremely thin at the top (fewer than 10 executives reporting to the CEO). The distinguishing feature is not the headcount distribution but the *number of layers*: 4-5 layers for 100,000+ employees is unusually flat by any standard.

*Strength of evidence:* Moderate. Chen Lin's account is based on internal sources. The "no titles" practice and minimal layers are widely reported across multiple sources. The specific span of control (15-25) is a single-source claim.

### Tencent: The Product-Centric "Federal" Structure

**Documented structure (Wu Xiaobo, *Tencent*, 2017, Chapter 10, pp. 255-265; Tencent annual reports):**

Tencent's hierarchy is less formally documented than Alibaba's or Huawei's, but Wu Xiaobo provides a structural description:

**The "federal" model:**

Wu Xiaobo (Chapter 10, p. 258) describes Tencent as operating as a "federation of product groups":

> "Tencent is not a single pyramid. It is multiple pyramids organized by product group. Each major product—WeChat, QQ, Games, Cloud, Video—operates with significant autonomy, its own hierarchy, its own culture. The group level is small and primarily allocates capital and sets strategic direction."

**Structure within product groups:**

Within each product group, the hierarchy is described as relatively flat by Chinese standards. Product teams have 3-4 layers from team lead to group VP. The WeChat group under Allen Zhang is famously lean: Wu Xiaobo reports that WeChat operated with fewer than 500 employees for its first several years, despite serving hundreds of millions of users—a ratio of employees to users that is extraordinarily low.

**The resulting shape:**

Tencent's overall hierarchy is best described as a **federation of pyramids**: multiple medium-sized hierarchies connected by a small corporate center. The product groups themselves are relatively flat; the overall company is multi-pyramid rather than single-pyramid.

*Strength of evidence:* Moderate. Wu Xiaobo's book is the primary source. Tencent's product group structure is publicly disclosed; the internal hierarchy within product groups is less formally documented.

### JD.com: The Tall, Command-Style Hierarchy

**Documented structure (Liu Qiangdong, *The JD.com Story*, 2016, Chapter 6, pp. 130-135):**

JD.com is the most hierarchically traditional of the documented firms. Liu Qiangdong describes a structure that is taller and more centralized than the others.

**Key documented features:**

Liu Qiangdong (Chapter 6, p. 132) describes JD.com's management structure:

> "JD.com has a clear chain of command. Every employee knows who they report to, who reports to them, and what decisions they can make. This is not a flat organization. It is a disciplined organization. Speed comes from clarity, not from ambiguity."

**The logistics hierarchy:**

JD.com's logistics workforce (the largest segment of its employees) operates in a tall, operations-style hierarchy:

- Frontline delivery and warehouse staff → Team leader → Station manager → Regional manager → Regional VP → National logistics VP → CEO

This is 6-7 layers from frontline to CEO for the logistics workforce—taller than ByteDance or Tencent, comparable to or slightly taller than Alibaba.

**The dual structure:**

JD.com operates with a dual structure similar to Huawei's: a tall formal hierarchy for steady-state operations, and a compressed campaign hierarchy during strategic initiatives (the 总指挥 model documented previously). The formal hierarchy is taller than any of the other documented firms except Huawei's; the campaign hierarchy is as flat as any of them.

**The resulting shape:**

JD.com's formal hierarchy is the most classically **pyramid-shaped** of the documented firms: a broad base of frontline and operational staff, a defined middle management layer, and a narrow top. The pyramid is steeper than Alibaba's (more layers) and much steeper than ByteDance's (much flatter).

*Strength of evidence:* Moderate. First-person account from the CEO. The logistics hierarchy is observable from JD.com's public organizational disclosures. The campaign compression is described by Liu Qiangdong and consistent with the campaign practices documented previously.

## 3. The "Where Are the Most People?" Question: Layer Distribution

Across all documented firms, the largest layer by headcount is consistently the base: individual contributors and frontline staff. This is true even at ByteDance, despite its flat hierarchy. The flatness is in the *number of layers*, not in the *distribution of people across layers*.

**Estimated headcount distribution by layer (synthesized from the sources above):**

| Layer | ByteDance | Alibaba | Huawei | JD.com |
|-------|-----------|---------|--------|--------|

| First-level managers | ~15-18% | ~20-22% | ~18-20% | ~15-18% |

## 4. The Diamond Question: Is There a "Bulging Middle"?

Some Western organizations develop a "diamond" shape where middle management is the largest layer—a consequence of title inflation, slow spans of control, and bureaucratic growth. The documented Chinese firms show no evidence of a diamond shape:

- **Alibaba's P8-P9 layer** (middle management equivalent) is approximately 20-28% of employees, substantially smaller than the P4-P7 base (65-75%)
- **Huawei's grade 19-21 layer** (middle management equivalent) is approximately 15-20%, compared to 65-75% for grades 13-18
- **ByteDance's deliberate flatness** actively prevents middle-management bulging by maintaining wide spans of control
- **JD.com's operations-heavy workforce** is naturally pyramid-shaped due to the large frontline logistics workforce

**The anti-diamond mechanisms:**

The documented firms have specific practices that prevent middle-management bloat:

1. **Wide spans of control:** ByteDance's 15-25 direct reports per manager; Alibaba's expectation that P8+ leaders manage significant teams
2. **Up-or-out pressure:** Huawei's forced distribution system pushes low performers out and prevents comfortable stagnation in middle layers
3. **Campaign compression:** The temporary suspension of hierarchy during campaigns reduces the need for permanent middle-management coordination roles
4. **Platform automation:** Alibaba's Middle Platform and Huawei's shared modules reduce the coordination work that middle managers typically perform, reducing the number of coordinators needed

## 5. The Dynamic Hierarchy: A Key Structural Insight

The most important finding for your article is not the static hierarchy shape but the *dynamic* nature of hierarchy in Chinese tech firms. The pattern across Huawei, Alibaba, JD.com, and (to a lesser extent) ByteDance is:

**Steady State:** A relatively tall, formal hierarchy for compensation, career progression, and day-to-day operations. This provides clarity, accountability, and career pathways.

**Campaign State:** A compressed, mission-oriented structure where formal rank is suspended, decision authority flows to the commander and frontline teams, and the hierarchy inverts if necessary. This provides speed, flexibility, and concentration of talent.

**The Transition Mechanism:** The formal declaration of a campaign (战役), the appointment of a commander (总指挥), and the issuance of transfer orders (调令) or resource requisition authority. These are the organizational switches that toggle between the two modes.

**The Reversion Mechanism:** Campaign closure, after-action review, and formal return to steady-state hierarchy. The reversion is as important as the transition—it prevents the campaign structure from becoming a permanent parallel hierarchy that would undermine the formal one.

## 6. The Article Implication

For a European employer, the structural insight is:

**Chinese tech firms achieve speed not by being permanently flat (which creates chaos at scale) or permanently tall (which creates slowness). They achieve speed by building a dual-mode hierarchy: tall and clear for steady-state, flat and fluid for campaigns. The organizational switches—campaign declaration, commander appointment, temporary authority suspension—are designed mechanisms, not cultural improvisations.**

This is directly transferable. A European organization can designate a strategic initiative as a "campaign" with a named commander, temporary authority to requisition resources, and permission to suspend normal reporting lines for the initiative's duration—with a clear reversion plan at the end. The key design elements are:

1. **Formal declaration** of the initiative as a campaign (not business-as-usual)
2. **Named commander** with documented temporary authority
3. **Compressed hierarchy** for the campaign team (3-4 layers max)
4. **Suspension of normal reporting lines** where they impede campaign speed
5. **Defined end date** and reversion to normal hierarchy

This achieves the speed of a flat organization without permanently abandoning the clarity of a hierarchical one.
