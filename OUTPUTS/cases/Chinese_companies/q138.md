## Company 1: Haier Group — Mixed Ownership, Radical Management Innovation

### Ownership Structure

Haier's ownership is the most complex of the three and has evolved significantly:

- **Origins:** Founded as a collectively-owned enterprise (集体企业 / Jítǐ Qǐyè) in 1984, when Zhang Ruimin took over a failing refrigerator factory in Qingdao
- **Current structure:** Haier Group is not a single legal entity but a complex ecosystem. The core listed entity is Haier Smart Home (海尔智家), listed on Shanghai, Hong Kong, and Frankfurt stock exchanges. Ownership is distributed among institutional investors, employee shareholding platforms, and entities with links to the Qingdao municipal government
- **Employee ownership:** A defining feature. Employees can become shareholders in the Xiaowei (micro-enterprises) they operate, creating direct ownership incentives
- **Zhang Ruimin's role:** Although officially retired as CEO (2021), Zhang remains the intellectual architect of the Rendanheyi model and retains significant influence
- **State connection:** The Qingdao municipal government retains influence through various entities, but Haier is not a traditional state-owned enterprise. It is best characterized as a "collective/employee-owned enterprise with state linkages"—a uniquely Chinese hybrid

**Source:** Haier Smart Home annual reports; Bill Fischer et al., *Reinventing Giants* (2013), Chapter 2, pp. 25-35, which details Haier's ownership evolution.

---

### Documented Execution Practices

**Rendanheyi (人单合一):** Haier's execution model is the most radical organizational experiment documented in this series. The key features:

- **Abolition of middle management (2013-2015):** Zhang Ruimin eliminated approximately 10,000 middle management positions, restructuring Haier into a platform supporting over 4,000 Xiaowei
- **Xiaowei autonomy:** Each Xiaowei operates with its own P&L, makes its own hiring and compensation decisions, and is directly accountable to customer orders
- **Internal market:** Xiaowei can "hire" shared services from the Haier platform (manufacturing, supply chain, HR, finance) or source them externally if the platform is not competitive
- **Ecosystem brand:** Since 2019, Haier has positioned itself as an "ecosystem brand"—a platform for entrepreneurs rather than a manufacturer of appliances

**Documented speed claim (Zhang Ruimin, HBR 2016):**

> "The time from identifying a user need to launching a product has been reduced from 18 months to as little as 3 months in some Xiaowei."

**Specific documented example:**

The "Lei Shen" (雷神 / Thunder God) gaming laptop brand is the most cited Haier Xiaowei success story. Three young Haier employees identified demand for gaming laptops through user community feedback. They formed a Xiaowei, developed the product, and launched within a timeframe significantly shorter than Haier's traditional product development cycle (approximately 6 months from concept to market, compared to 12-18 months for traditional Haier product lines). Lei Shen became a top-three gaming laptop brand in China within three years.

**Source:** Bill Fischer et al., *Reinventing Giants* (2013), Chapter 7, pp. 155-165; Haier case study, Harvard Business School, 2018.

---

### Strengths of the Ownership Structure for Execution

- **Employee ownership incentives:** Xiaowei members who hold equity are directly financially motivated to execute quickly and well. This aligns incentives more strongly than salary-based compensation.
- **Decentralized decision-making:** The Xiaowei model pushes decisions to the frontline, eliminating the hierarchical approval delays that slow execution in traditional firms.
- **Market discipline:** Xiaowei must earn their revenue from customers, not receive it from a corporate budget. Underperforming Xiaowei are not "restructured"—they lose revenue and must improve or disband. This is a harder accountability mechanism than internal performance reviews.
- **Platform leverage without platform bureaucracy:** Xiaowei can access Haier's manufacturing, supply chain, and distribution capabilities without waiting for corporate approvals—but they can also go outside if the platform is slow.

---

### Constraints of the Ownership Structure

- **Coordination complexity:** With 4,000+ Xiaowei, coordination is a genuine challenge. The platform must enforce standards and enable interoperability without undermining Xiaowei autonomy.
- **Brand fragmentation risk:** Multiple Xiaowei under the Haier umbrella could create brand inconsistency if platform standards are not enforced.
- **Governance ambiguity:** The mixed ownership structure creates complex governance. Who has ultimate authority? Zhang Ruimin's personal authority held the system together; his retirement creates uncertainty about whether the model is institutionalized or leader-dependent.
- **Scalability of the model:** The Xiaowei model works well for discrete products (gaming laptop, air conditioner model) but is less proven for complex, integrated systems that require deep cross-unit coordination.

## Company 2: Midea Group — Private, Founder-Controlled, Disciplined Transformation

### Ownership Structure

- **Founder and controlling shareholder:** He Xiangjian (何享健), who founded Midea in 1968, remains the controlling shareholder through his holding company, Midea Holding
- **Public listing:** Midea Group is listed on the Shenzhen Stock Exchange (000333.SZ)
- **Professional management:** In 2012, He Xiangjian handed the CEO role to Paul Fang (方洪波), a career Midea executive, marking one of China's most successful founder-to-professional-manager transitions
- **Ownership concentration:** He Xiangjian's family retains effective control while allowing significant public float. This is a classic "founder-controlled public company" structure, similar to many Western family-controlled firms.

**Source:** Midea Group annual reports; Gereffi et al., Duke GVC Center case study (2018).

---

### Documented Execution Practices

**T+3 Transformation (2012-2015):**

As documented in detail earlier in this series, Midea's T+3 system is the most thoroughly documented operational transformation in the Chinese home appliance sector:

- **T (order placement) → T+1 (materials) → T+2 (production) → T+3 (delivery)**
- **Shift from build-to-stock to build-to-order**
- **Inventories reduced from 51 days (2012) to 29 days (2016)**
- **Warehouses reduced from 2,200+ to under 500**
- **Order-to-delivery cycle reduced from 23 days to 9 days**

**Key enabler:** Paul Fang's leadership as a professional CEO with founder backing. The transformation was implemented with a speed and comprehensiveness that reflects the combination of professional management discipline and founder authority.

**Source:** Gereffi et al., 2018, pp. 14-18; Midea 2013-2016 annual reports.

---

### Strengths of the Ownership Structure for Execution

- **Founder authority without founder micromanagement:** He Xiangjian's continued ownership stake gives Paul Fang the backing to make bold decisions (like the T+3 transformation, which required significant short-term investment). But Fang has operational autonomy—a structure that combines strategic stability with execution agility.
- **Professional management culture:** Fang is a career professional, not a founder. He has built a management system that emphasizes data, process discipline, and performance accountability—not personal authority.
- **Clear accountability:** As a publicly listed company with a controlling shareholder, Midea has clear lines of accountability: Fang to the board, the board to shareholders. There is no ambiguity about who decides what.
- **Access to capital markets:** The public listing provides access to capital for investment and acquisition (including Midea's 2016 acquisition of KUKA, the German robotics company), without state interference.

---

### Constraints of the Ownership Structure

- **Succession risk:** The model depends on He Xiangjian's family continuing to support professional management. A future generational transition could disrupt this.
- **Short-term market pressure:** As a listed company, Midea faces quarterly earnings pressure that privately-held or state-owned competitors may not.
- **No Xiaowei-level entrepreneurial incentive:** Unlike Haier's employee ownership model, Midea employees are employees. The incentives are strong (performance-based compensation, promotion) but are not equity-level incentives at the front line.
- **Less radical experimentation:** Midea's T+3 is an excellent operational transformation, but it is within the traditional corporate structure. Midea has not attempted anything as radical as Rendanheyi.

## Company 3: Hisense Group — State-Owned Enterprise, Centralized Governance

### Ownership Structure

- **Controlling shareholder:** Hisense Group is controlled by the Qingdao Municipal State-owned Assets Supervision and Administration Commission (SASAC), the same government entity that historically had links to Haier
- **Listed subsidiary:** Hisense Visual Technology (600060.SH) is listed on the Shanghai Stock Exchange; Hisense Home Appliances (000921.SZ) is listed on the Shenzhen Stock Exchange
- **Party Committee governance:** As an SOE, Hisense operates under the "Party Committee leadership" model, where the Party Committee within the company has formal authority over major strategic decisions
- **Government-appointed leadership:** Top executives are effectively appointed by the Qingdao municipal government, not selected by an independent board

**Source:** Hisense annual reports; Chinese corporate governance regulations for SOEs.

---

### Documented Execution Practices

**Transformation efforts:**

Hisense has pursued digital transformation and global expansion, but with less documented radicalism than Haier or Midea:

- **Global brand building:** Hisense has invested heavily in global brand recognition, including sponsorship of FIFA World Cup (2018, 2022) and UEFA European Championship. This represents significant resource allocation to a long-term strategic initiative.
- **Technology investment:** Hisense has invested in display technology (laser TV, ULED) and chip design for TVs, building in-house capabilities in strategic areas.
- **Acquisition strategy:** Hisense acquired Toshiba's TV business (2017) and Gorenje (Slovenian appliance manufacturer, 2018), pursuing international expansion through acquisition.

**Speed comparison:**

Hisense does not publish transformation metrics comparable to Midea's T+3 results or Haier's Xiaowei launch timelines. The company is profitable and growing, but its execution speed is not documented as exceptional in the way that Haier's and Midea's are.

**Source:** Hisense annual reports; *Caixin* and *Yicai Global* reporting on Hisense's international expansion.

---

### Strengths of the Ownership Structure for Execution

- **Strategic patience:** As an SOE, Hisense faces less pressure for quarterly earnings and can pursue long-term investments (World Cup sponsorship, laser TV technology) that might be harder to justify in a public company facing activist investors.
- **Access to state resources:** Hisense has preferential access to state bank lending, government procurement contracts, and policy support that private competitors may not enjoy.
- **Stability:** State ownership provides a stability that private firms lack. Hisense will not face a hostile takeover, a founder succession crisis, or a liquidity crunch driven by market sentiment.
- **Scale in protected segments:** In certain government-adjacent market segments (public procurement, state-owned infrastructure projects), Hisense's SOE status is an advantage.

---

### Constraints of the Ownership Structure for Execution

- **Bureaucratic governance:** The Party Committee governance model and SASAC oversight create multiple layers of approval. Strategic decisions that at Midea can be made by Paul Fang and at Haier by Xiaowei leaders require committee review and government alignment at Hisense.
- **Limited employee incentives:** SOE compensation structures are constrained. Hisense cannot offer the equity incentives that Haier's Xiaowei model provides or the performance bonuses that Midea's professional management system offers. This limits the intensity of individual motivation.
- **Risk aversion:** SOE executives face asymmetric risk: failure is personally costly (career damage, potential Party discipline), while extraordinary success does not bring commensurate personal reward (compensation caps). This creates a structural bias toward caution.
- **Slower decision-making:** The need for government alignment on major strategic decisions inevitably slows execution compared to founder-controlled or mixed-ownership peers.
- **Talent competition:** Hisense competes for talent with Haier (same city, Qingdao) and other Chinese tech firms. The SOE compensation constraints make this competition difficult.

**Documented evidence of the constraint:**

A 2019 *Caixin* analysis of Chinese home appliance SOEs noted:

> "State-owned home appliance companies face a structural disadvantage in the competition for digital talent. A product manager who can earn equity in a Haier Xiaowei or a performance bonus at Midea has limited upside at an SOE where compensation bands are regulated. The best talent flows to the companies that offer the strongest incentives."

— *Caixin*, "Home Appliance SOEs and the Talent Gap," 2019

## Comparative Analysis: Ownership and Execution Speed

### The Central Finding

**Haier (mixed ownership) and Midea (private) both execute faster than Hisense (SOE), but they achieve speed through different mechanisms.** Ownership structure matters, but it matters through its effect on management design choices, not as a direct determinant of speed.

### Execution Speed by Dimension

| Dimension | Haier (Mixed) | Midea (Private) | Hisense (SOE) |
|-----------|---------------|-----------------|---------------|
| **Product development speed** | Very high (3-6 months for Xiaowei launches) | High (T+3: order-to-delivery in 9 days) | Moderate (no documented exceptional speed) |
| **Strategic decision-making speed** | Distributed (Xiaowei decide) | Concentrated but delegated (Fang decides, with founder backing) | Slow (multi-layer approval, Party Committee, SASAC) |
| **Resource reallocation speed** | Market-driven (Xiaowei earn or lose resources based on customer revenue) | Management-driven (T+3 enforced from top) | Bureaucratic (annual budget cycle, government alignment) |
| **Talent mobilization speed** | High (Xiaowei leaders recruit directly) | High (professional management system) | Low (compensation constraints, approval processes) |
| **Failure tolerance (exploration)** | High (Xiaowei failure is market failure; entrepreneurs move on) | Moderate (structured accountability; less tolerance for repeated failure) | Low (asymmetric risk for executives) |
| **Failure intolerance (execution)** | High (Xiaowei that fail to earn revenue are not subsidized) | Very high (T+3 has hard consequences for misses) | Moderate (bureaucratic consequences, not market consequences) |

---

### What Explains the Variance?

**Ownership structure explains the constraints and incentives within which management operates.** But within those constraints, management design choices determine actual execution speed:

1. **Haier's mixed ownership enabled the radical Rendanheyi experiment.** A purely private company might not have attempted something so radical (it risks the brand and the existing business). A purely state-owned company could not have attempted it (employee ownership and abolition of hierarchy would violate SOE governance norms). The hybrid structure—collective origins, employee ownership, partial state linkage—created a unique space for Zhang Ruimin's experiment.

2. **Midea's private ownership enabled disciplined, top-down transformation.** Paul Fang could enforce T+3 across the entire organization because he had founder backing and clear authority. An SOE executive could not have moved as decisively. A Haier Xiaowei leader could not impose cross-unit standards. Midea's ownership structure is optimal for a certain kind of transformation: centrally designed, comprehensively enforced, performance-accountable.

3. **Hisense's SOE structure imposes a speed ceiling.** Hisense is not a poorly managed company. It is profitable, global, and investing in technology. But its ownership structure creates a ceiling on execution speed that no management design can fully overcome. The multi-layer approval process, asymmetric executive risk, and talent competition constraints are structural, not cultural.

---

### The Implication: Ownership Is a Constraint, Not a Determinant

**The documented evidence supports a nuanced conclusion:**

- **Private ownership (Midea) enables disciplined, top-down execution speed** when combined with professional management, founder backing, and clear accountability structures.
- **Mixed ownership (Haier) enables radical, bottom-up execution speed** when combined with visionary leadership, employee ownership incentives, and platform architecture.
- **State ownership (Hisense) imposes a speed ceiling** that constrains even well-managed companies. SOEs can be profitable, global, and technologically capable, but they cannot match the execution speed of private or mixed-ownership peers because their governance structure imposes a higher coordination cost and a lower risk tolerance.

**The most important finding for your article:**

The variance between Haier and Midea—both fast, but through different mechanisms—shows that there is no single "Chinese ownership model" that produces speed. Haier's speed comes from radical decentralization and market discipline. Midea's speed comes from disciplined centralization and process enforcement. Both work. Both are faster than the SOE alternative. The common factor is not ownership structure per se, but the *management choice to invest in a coherent execution architecture* (Rendanheyi for Haier, T+3 for Midea) and the *ownership structure's willingness to permit that choice*.

**For a European employer, the lesson is:** Do not fixate on ownership structure. A family-controlled company, a publicly listed company, a foundation-owned company, or a private equity-backed company can all execute quickly—if they make the architectural and management investments documented in this series. The question is not "what is your ownership structure?" but "does your ownership structure permit you to make the management choices that produce speed?"
