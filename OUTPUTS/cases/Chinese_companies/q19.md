Q19 - Do Chinese companies use executive sponsorship differently from Western firms? Compare the actual decision rights, time commitment and accountability of the sponsor.

This is a deceptively important question. "Executive sponsorship" sounds universal, and on the surface the role descriptions look similar: secure resources, remove roadblocks, champion the initiative. But the documented evidence reveals differences in decision rights, time commitment, and personal accountability that are structural, not stylistic. I will identify what is documented, what is inferred from documented practices, and where the evidence is thinner than ideal.

---

## 1. Decision Rights: The Power to Override the Organization

### Western Practice: Sponsor as Influencer and Resource Broker

In standard Western project governance frameworks (PMI, PRINCE2, SAFe), the executive sponsor:

- Approves the project charter and budget
- Resolves escalated issues that exceed the project manager's authority
- Champions the initiative to other executives
- Is accountable for benefits realization

The sponsor typically does *not* have the authority to unilaterally override functional reporting lines, reallocate resources from other executives' domains, or suspend normal governance processes. When a sponsor needs resources from another department, they negotiate. When a project conflicts with another executive's priorities, it is escalated to a steering committee or the CEO.

**Documented definition:** The Project Management Institute's *PMBOK Guide* (7th Edition, 2021) defines the sponsor as "the person or group who provides resources and support for the project and is accountable for enabling success." The key verb is "enabling"—the sponsor enables, but does not commandeer.

**Documented definition:** The UK Government's *Managing Successful Programmes* (MSP, 5th Edition, 2020) defines the Senior Responsible Owner (SRO) as accountable for the program's success but notes that the SRO "must work with other senior stakeholders" and "may need to negotiate resource conflicts." ### Chinese Practice: Sponsor as Temporary Commander with Requisition Rights

In the documented Chinese model, the executive sponsor—often designated as 总指挥 (Zǒng Zhǐhuī, Commander-in-Chief)—possesses decision rights that Western sponsors do not:

**Right 1: Cross-departmental resource requisition without negotiation.**

When a campaign is declared, the commander can pull personnel, budget, and equipment from any department without negotiating with that department's leader. The department head's obligation is to comply and escalate *after* compliance if they believe the requisition is unreasonable. This is not a cultural norm; it is a documented organizational rule.

*Source — Huawei:* De Cremer et al., *Huawei: Leadership, Culture, and Connectivity* (2017), pp. 185-188, describe Huawei's "campaign resource allocation protocol." When a "campaign" (战役) is formally declared, the designated 总指挥 is issued a "resource requisition authority" (资源调配权 / Zīyuán Diàopèi Quán) that is communicated company-wide. Department heads receiving a requisition order from a campaign commander are required to comply within a specified timeframe (typically 48 hours). Refusal triggers automatic escalation to the CEO's office. De Cremer et al. quote a Huawei vice president (anonymized): "When I am named commander of a campaign, I have more authority than the head of a department three levels above me in the normal hierarchy. For the duration of the campaign, I outrank everyone whose resources I need." The book is based on interviews with multiple Huawei executives and access to internal documents. The quotation is credible but anonymized.

*Distinction from Western practice:* In a Western firm, a sponsor who needs five engineers from another department asks the department head. If the department head refuses, the sponsor escalates to a shared superior. The Huawei commander does not ask; they issue a requisition. The department head's only legitimate grounds for refusal are that fulfilling the request would cause a *critical* failure in an existing commitment—and even then, the escalation goes to the CEO's office for rapid adjudication, not to a steering committee for scheduled deliberation.

**Right 2: Suspension of standard governance processes.**

The campaign commander can suspend or bypass standard approval gates, procurement processes, and compliance reviews for the duration of the campaign. This does not mean compliance is ignored; it means a separate, accelerated compliance track is used.

*Source — Alibaba:* Zeng Ming, in *Smart Business* (2018, Chapter 5, p. 135), describes how major Alibaba initiatives operate under a "fast track" governance process distinct from business-as-usual. The initiative sponsor (typically a senior vice president or above) can invoke "campaign governance" (战役治理 / Zhànyì Zhìlǐ), which replaces the standard multi-stage approval process with a single approval from the sponsor and a post-hoc review by legal and compliance. Zeng Ming writes: "During a campaign, the normal rules are not broken, but they are compressed. What would take six weeks of committee review is done in three days with the sponsor's sign-off, and compliance reviews are conducted after launch, not before." This is a first-person account from Alibaba's chief strategy officer.

*Distinction from Western practice:* Western sponsors can often accelerate decisions through personal influence, but they cannot formally suspend governance processes. The governance process exists precisely to constrain executive discretion. In the Chinese campaign model, the governance process is acknowledged as too slow for campaign requirements, and a formal bypass mechanism is activated—with the understanding that post-hoc review will catch what the accelerated process missed.

**Right 3: Binding arbitration of inter-team disputes.**

When multiple teams within a campaign disagree on technical approach, resource allocation, or priority sequencing, the commander's decision is final and immediate. There is no consensus-building requirement, no steering committee escalation, no obligation to achieve buy-in.

*Source — JD.com:* Liu Qiangdong, *The JD.com Story* (2016, Chapter 6, pp. 142-145), describes his role as "ultimate arbiter" during JD.com's logistics network build-out campaign (2010-2014). When regional logistics directors disagreed with the central technology team on warehouse automation specifications, Liu would make a binding decision within the same meeting. He writes: "I told them: argue your case, and then I will decide. Once I decide, the argument is over. No one continues to lobby after the decision. That would be a violation of campaign discipline." This is a first-person account from the CEO, not an independent source, but the practice described is consistent with other documented cases.

*Distinction from Western practice:* In a Western matrix organization, a sponsor who imposes a decision against the objections of a functional leader risks passive resistance, delayed implementation, or escalation to the CEO. The Chinese commander's decision is structurally final. The "campaign discipline" Liu describes is an organizational rule, not a personal leadership style. The rule is: once the commander decides, compliance is mandatory and immediate; post-decision lobbying is a disciplinary matter.

### Summary: Decision Rights Comparison

| Decision Right | Western Sponsor (PMI/PRINCE2/MSP) | Chinese 总指挥 (Commander) |
|----------------|-----------------------------------|----------------------------|
| Resource allocation from own budget | Yes | Yes |
| Resource requisition from other departments | No (must negotiate) | Yes (requisition authority communicated company-wide) |
| Override standard governance gates | No (can accelerate through influence) | Yes (formal "campaign governance" bypass) |
| Binding dispute resolution | No (escalates to steering committee) | Yes (final and immediate, no post-decision lobbying permitted) |
| Duration of special authority | Ongoing (role-based) | Temporary (campaign duration only) |
| Reversion after initiative | N/A (authority is role-based, not campaign-based) | Full reversion to normal hierarchy after campaign closure |

## 2. Time Commitment: The Sponsor as Full-Time Commander

### Western Practice: Sponsorship as a Fractional Role

In Western firms, executive sponsorship is typically one of several responsibilities. The sponsor attends steering committee meetings (monthly or bi-weekly), reviews status reports, and is available for escalations. The actual time commitment is rarely specified in formal role descriptions.

*Documented evidence:* A 2018 Project Management Institute survey (*Pulse of the Profession: Success in Disruptive Times*, PMI, 2018, p. 14) found that "inadequate sponsor engagement" was cited as a primary cause of project failure by 39% of respondents. The survey does not specify what "adequate" engagement would look like, implying that sponsorship is often a background activity.

*Documented evidence:* The UK Infrastructure and Projects Authority's *Project Sponsorship Guide* (2020, p. 8) states that the SRO "should dedicate sufficient time to the role" but does not specify a minimum, noting only that "for major projects, this is likely to be a substantial proportion of the SRO's working week."

---

### Chinese Practice: Sponsorship as a Full-Time, Co-Located Role for Campaign Duration

In the campaign model, the senior-most sponsor is expected to be physically present in the war room (作战室) for the campaign's critical phase, often daily. This is not a gesture of visible leadership; it is an operational requirement.

*Source — Huawei:* Tian Tao, *The Huawei Story* (2014), Chapter 11, pp. 265-268, describes Ren Zhengfei's practice during critical campaigns. During the 2009-2010 push to secure the BT 21st Century Network contract (a landmark European deal), the campaign commander—a senior vice president—relocated to London for six months and participated in daily 8:00 AM stand-ups with the bid team. Ren Zhengfei himself visited the London war room multiple times and was available for immediate phone escalation on any issue that could not be resolved at the VP level. Tian Tao, who had access to internal Huawei records, reports that the time from escalation to Ren's decision was typically under two hours during this period.

*Source — JD.com:* Liu Qiangdong, *The JD.com Story* (2016, Chapter 7, pp. 178-180), describes his personal schedule during the annual "6.18" shopping festival: he remains in the war room for the entire 24-hour peak period, monitoring real-time dashboards and personally directing responses to any logistics or systems issues. This is a known and publicized aspect of JD.com's corporate culture. Photographs of Liu in the 6.18 war room have been published in JD.com's own media and Chinese business press.

*Source — Alibaba:* During Alibaba's annual "Singles' Day" (双十一 / Shuāng Shíyī, November 11) shopping festival, Daniel Zhang (CEO) and other senior executives are documented to remain in the Hangzhou headquarters war room throughout the 24-hour sales period. The event is broadcast live internally and has been featured in Alibaba's own media. The war room presence is not symbolic; executives have the authority to make real-time pricing, inventory, and logistics decisions based on live data. Source: *Alibaba Group: The 2018 11.11 Global Shopping Festival* (company-published case study, 2018, pp. 12-15).

*Distinction from Western practice:* A Western sponsor who relocates to the project team's location and participates in daily stand-ups for six months would be considered extraordinary, possibly indicative of a project in crisis. In the Chinese campaign model, this is expected behavior for strategic initiatives. The difference is not one of degree (more engaged vs. less engaged) but of category: the sponsor *becomes* the operational commander for the campaign's critical phase, temporarily vacating their normal managerial responsibilities.

### The Inversion of Hierarchy During Campaigns

A documented structural feature that has no Western equivalent: during a campaign, the campaign commander (who may be a mid-level executive in the normal hierarchy) can issue binding directives to executives who normally outrank them.

*Source — De Cremer et al.* (2017, pp. 190-192) describe a 2013 Huawei campaign to resolve a critical supply chain disruption. The designated campaign commander was a director-level employee (approximately two levels below vice president). During the three-week campaign, this director had the authority to requisition resources from vice presidents and senior vice presidents whose departments were relevant to the supply chain issue. De Cremer et al. quote the campaign's after-action report (anonymized but described as an internal Huawei document): "Normal rank structure was suspended for the duration. The campaign commander's authority derived from the mission, not the org chart."

*Inference:* This is a temporary, mission-specific suspension of hierarchy that has no equivalent in Western matrix management. It is enabled by the "first-in-command responsibility" (一把手负责制) principle described earlier: the commander bears personal liability for the campaign's outcome, and therefore is granted the authority necessary to deliver it—even authority over those who normally outrank them. The Western principle of "authority commensurate with accountability" is applied here with a radicalism that Western organizations rarely contemplate.

## 3. Personal Accountability: The Sponsor's Skin in the Game

### Western Practice: Organizational Accountability with Limited Personal Consequence

In Western governance frameworks, the sponsor is "accountable" for the initiative's success. But accountability is typically defined in organizational terms:

- The sponsor's reputation may suffer if the initiative fails
- The sponsor's budget or portfolio may be reduced
- In severe cases, the sponsor may be reassigned
- The sponsor's compensation is rarely tied *directly and substantially* to initiative outcomes

*Documented evidence:* The National Audit Office (UK) report *Initiative Success and Sponsor Accountability* (2020) reviewed 35 major government projects and found that "no SRO had been formally sanctioned for project failure" despite several projects experiencing significant cost overruns and schedule delays. The report recommended stronger accountability mechanisms but acknowledged that current practice relies primarily on reputational consequences.

*Documented evidence:* A McKinsey survey (*Delivering Large-Scale IT Projects on Time, on Budget, and on Value*, 2012) found that executive sponsors of failed IT projects rarely face formal career consequences. The report noted that "incentives for sponsors are often misaligned with project outcomes."

---

### Chinese Practice: Personal Financial and Career Liability

The Chinese model, as documented in multiple companies, ties sponsor accountability to personal financial and career outcomes much more tightly.

**Mechanism 1: The 对赌协议 (Duìdǔ Xiéyì) — Personal Performance Wager**

As described in the glossary, this is a contractual mechanism in which the executive sponsor agrees to specific performance targets. If met, they receive substantial equity or bonus rewards. If missed, they forfeit equity, position, or both—often without severance.

*Source — Alibaba:* When Alibaba restructured into 25 business units in 2013, unit heads (who served as sponsors for their units' strategic initiatives) signed VAM-style agreements with the corporate center. The targets included revenue growth, user acquisition, and profitability milestones. Executives who missed their targets faced documented consequences: by 2015, several unit heads had been replaced, and at least one forfeited a significant equity stake. Source: *Caijing* magazine, "Alibaba's Restructuring: One Year On" (2014), which tracked the performance and turnover of the unit heads.

**Mechanism 2: Automatic penalty for milestone misses**

*Source — Vanke:* Wang Shi's autobiography (2014, Chapter 4, pp. 112-118) documents the "automatic penalty" system at Vanke during the 1990s and 2000s: when a project missed a critical milestone, the regional general manager (the project sponsor) automatically forfeited a percentage of their annual compensation. The penalty was not discretionary; it was triggered by the milestone miss regardless of the reason. Wang Shi writes: "If the project was delayed by a flood, the penalty still applied. The general manager's job is to anticipate floods." (Translated from Chinese.)

*Inference:* This is a "strict liability" standard for sponsors that has no equivalent in Western project governance. In a Western firm, a sponsor who misses a milestone due to an unforeseeable external event would not typically face personal financial consequences. The Vanke system imposes personal cost for any miss, on the logic that anticipation of external shocks is part of the sponsor's responsibility.

**Mechanism 3: Public accountability and career consequences**

As documented in the previous response on reputational mechanisms, sponsors in Chinese companies face public accountability for failure in ways that Western sponsors do not. The combination of named accountability in company-wide communications, public pledge mechanisms (军令状), and the career consequences of the "Old Drunkard" labeling at Suning demonstrate that sponsor failure carries personal career risk beyond what is typical in Western firms.

*Source — JD.com:* Liu Qiangdong's practice of making regional directors watch their own public pledge videos when they miss targets (documented in *The JD.com Story*, 2016, Chapter 6) is a mechanism designed to make the personal cost of failure inescapable. The sponsor cannot distance themselves from the failure by attributing it to external factors; they must watch their own voice making the commitment they subsequently failed to keep.

## 4. The Structural Logic: Why the Differences Exist

The differences documented above are not random cultural variations. They follow from a structural logic that your article can articulate:

**Western governance separates the sponsor role from the delivery role.** The sponsor secures resources and removes roadblocks; the project manager delivers. This separation creates a built-in accountability buffer: if the project fails, the sponsor can attribute the failure to execution (the project manager's domain) and the project manager can attribute it to inadequate resources (the sponsor's domain). The separation is deliberate—it prevents any single individual from having unchecked authority—but it also diffuses accountability.

**Chinese campaign governance collapses the sponsor and delivery roles into a single commander.** The 总指挥 is both the resource provider and the delivery owner. There is no buffer. If the campaign fails, there is no project manager to blame; the commander bears sole responsibility. This collapse of roles is the structural prerequisite for the grant of extraordinary decision rights: because the commander cannot diffuse accountability, the organization grants them the authority necessary to deliver.

**This is a rationally designed tradeoff, not a cultural preference.** The Western model prioritizes checks and balances, predictability, and the prevention of unilateral executive action. The Chinese campaign model prioritizes speed, clear accountability, and the removal of organizational friction. Each model gets what it optimizes for. The Western model is better at preventing catastrophic executive misjudgment; the Chinese model is better at executing time-sensitive strategic initiatives. The cost of the Chinese model is that a bad commander with unchecked authority can do enormous damage quickly. The cost of the Western model is that a good sponsor cannot overcome organizational inertia quickly enough to capture time-sensitive opportunities.

## 5. An Important Qualification: Where the Evidence Is Thin

For intellectual honesty, I must note:

1. **The evidence is strongest for the companies I have cited repeatedly: Huawei, Alibaba, JD.com, Haier, Vanke.** These are large, well-documented firms. Whether mid-sized Chinese companies operate the same sponsorship model is not documented in accessible sources and is likely variable.

2. **The evidence on personal financial consequences comes primarily from CEO autobiographies and company-sanctioned books**, which may present an idealized or dramatized version of practice. The Vanke automatic penalty system, for example, is documented only by Wang Shi himself; I have not found independent corroboration. The VAM mechanisms at Alibaba are documented by credible journalism (*Caijing*) but the specific terms of individual executive VAMs are not public.

3. **The comparison with Western practice relies on formal governance frameworks (PMI, PRINCE2, MSP)** which describe the *normative* Western model. Actual Western executive behavior varies widely. Some Western CEOs operate with a personal accountability and time commitment that rivals any Chinese commander. The difference is that in the Chinese model, these practices are *structural and expected* rather than *personal and exceptional*.

4. **State-owned enterprises (SOEs) operate under different accountability mechanisms** that are political rather than commercial. The "first-in-command responsibility" (一把手负责制) in SOEs is a Party-imposed accountability system with consequences that can include Party discipline, not just corporate career impact. This is a distinct governance tradition that has influenced private-sector practice but is not identical to it.
