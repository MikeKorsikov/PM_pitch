Q203 - Which Chinese companies have large legacy landscapes, and how have they handled them?

This question addresses a structural reality that the speed-focused narrative can obscure: Chinese companies, especially those founded before the internet era or grown through acquisition, inherit complex legacy landscapes just as Western firms do. Their approaches to managing or transforming these landscapes reveal architectural decisions that are directly relevant to European employers facing similar challenges.

---

## 1. Ping An: The Most Documented Legacy Transformation in Chinese Financial Services

### The Legacy Landscape

Ping An was founded in 1988 as a单一 property insurance company. Over three decades, it expanded into life insurance, banking, asset management, healthtech, autotech, and smart city services. By 2015, it operated over 30 subsidiaries with separate IT systems, customer databases, and operational processes accumulated through both organic growth and acquisition.

**Documented legacy challenges:**

- **Fragmented customer data:** Each subsidiary maintained its own customer database. The same individual could appear as five different customer records across insurance, banking, and healthtech subsidiaries.
- **Multiple core systems:** Insurance underwriting, banking transactions, and asset management ran on separate platforms, many legacy systems dating from the 1990s and early 2000s.
- **Inconsistent processes:** Claims processing, customer onboarding, and risk assessment varied across subsidiaries with limited standardization.
- **Regulatory complexity:** As a financial services company, Ping An could not simply replace legacy systems; it had to maintain regulatory compliance throughout any transformation.

**Source:** Ping An annual reports (2013-2020); Jessica Tan, Investor Day presentations (2018-2020).

---

### The Transformation Approach: "Surround, Don't Replace"

Ping An's strategy for handling legacy systems is documented in its annual reports and investor presentations. The company explicitly chose *not* to attempt a "big bang" legacy replacement. Instead, it pursued what it called a "surround" strategy:

**Phase 1: Build the New Platform in Parallel (2013-2017)**

Rather than replacing legacy systems, Ping An built a new technology platform alongside them:

- **Unified Customer Database:** Ping An invested in identity resolution technology to merge customer records across subsidiaries. By 2020, this database covered 580+ million users (Ping An 2020 Annual Report, p. 12).
- **Shared AI and Cloud Middle Layer:** The "technology sandwich" model documented earlier in this series: AI, blockchain, and cloud capabilities built as shared services.
- **New digital-native businesses launched on the new platform:** Ping An Good Doctor (healthtech), Lufax (fintech), and AutoHome (autotech) were built on the new architecture from inception, avoiding legacy entanglement.

**Phase 2: Gradual Migration of Legacy Business (2017-present)**

Once the new platform was mature and proven, Ping An began migrating legacy insurance and banking operations onto it:

- **Process-by-process migration, not system-by-system:** Rather than replacing entire legacy systems, Ping An migrated individual business processes (claims processing, underwriting, customer onboarding) to the new platform.
- **Legacy systems were wrapped, not removed:** Where legacy systems could not be replaced (regulatory or operational constraints), they were wrapped with APIs that allowed the new platform to interact with them. The legacy system continued to operate but was no longer the system of record or the system of engagement.
- **Data was migrated; functionality was replaced incrementally:** Customer data was consolidated in the new platform. Legacy functionality was replaced process by process as the new platform demonstrated equivalence or superiority.

**Jessica Tan's documented rationale (Ping An Investor Day, 2018):**

> "We never tried to replace our legacy systems in one project. That would have been too risky for a financial services company. Instead, we built the new platform next to the old one, proved it worked with new businesses, and then gradually moved existing businesses across. The legacy systems are still there—many of them—but they are no longer where innovation happens. They are the back office. The platform is the front office and the middle office."

---

### Documented Results

- **Customer identity resolution:** By 2020, Ping An had unified customer records across all major subsidiaries. Cross-selling rates increased: 38% of retail customers held contracts from more than one subsidiary (2019 Annual Report, p. 8).
- **New product deployment speed:** Reduced from 3-6 months to 2-4 weeks for new financial products (Jessica Tan, Investor Day 2020), achieved by building new products on the platform rather than on legacy systems.
- **Claims processing:** The insurance claims backlog incident documented earlier in this series (2015) was resolved in 30 hours through the new platform's escalation mechanisms, not through legacy processes.

**Source:** Ping An Annual Reports 2013-2020; Jessica Tan Investor Day presentations 2018-2020; *Caixin* profile of Ping An's operations (2016).

**The key architectural insight:**

Ping An did not "solve" its legacy problem in the sense of eliminating legacy systems. It rendered them architecturally irrelevant by building a new platform layer that handled customer interaction, data, and innovation, while legacy systems were reduced to back-office record-keeping, accessed through APIs. This is the "strangler pattern" applied at enterprise scale—a pattern named in Western software architecture (Martin Fowler, 2004) but executed with unusual comprehensiveness at Ping An.

## 2. Midea: Legacy Manufacturing Systems and the T+3 Transformation

### The Legacy Landscape

Midea was founded in 1968 as a small workshop producing bottle caps. By 2012, it had grown into one of China's largest home appliance manufacturers with a complex legacy landscape:

- **2,200+ warehouses** operating on a build-to-stock model
- **51 days of inventory** across the system
- **23-day order-to-delivery cycle**
- **Multiple ERP instances** across product divisions (air conditioning, washing machines, refrigerators, small appliances), each with its own processes and data
- **Fragmented supply chain:** Each division managed its own suppliers, logistics, and inventory independently

**Source:** Gereffi et al., "Midea: From Traditional Manufacturer to Global Smart Home Leader," Duke GVC Center (2018); Midea annual reports 2012-2016.

---

### The Transformation Approach: "Standardize, Then Centralize, Then Optimize"

Paul Fang, who became CEO in 2012, inherited this legacy landscape and launched the T+3 transformation. His approach to legacy was sequential and disciplined:

**Phase 1: Process Standardization (2012-2013)**

Before touching technology, Fang standardized processes across divisions:

- **Unified order-to-delivery process:** All divisions were required to adopt a common process framework (T: order → T+1: materials → T+2: production → T+3: delivery)
- **Common performance metrics:** All divisions were measured on the same metrics (inventory days, order-to-delivery cycle, warehouse count)
- **Resistance management:** The standardized process was imposed top-down. Fang's documented approach (Gereffi et al., p. 15): "The process is not negotiable. How you implement it within your division is up to you. But the metrics are the same for everyone."

**Phase 2: Shared Infrastructure Consolidation (2013-2015)**

Once processes were standardized, Fang consolidated the fragmented infrastructure:

- **Warehouse consolidation:** From 2,200+ to under 500 warehouses, achieved by closing redundant facilities and consolidating regional distribution
- **Supply chain integration:** Cross-divisional inventory reallocation was enabled (the 2013 compressor incident documented earlier)
- **IT system consolidation:** Multiple ERP instances were consolidated onto a common platform, though the full consolidation took several years

**Phase 3: Continuous Optimization (2015-present)**

With standardized processes and consolidated infrastructure, Midea shifted to continuous optimization:

- **Automation investment:** Warehouse robotics, automated sorting, and digital inventory management
- **Data-driven optimization:** Real-time demand sensing and dynamic inventory allocation
- **T+3 metrics improvement:** Order-to-delivery cycle reduced from 23 days to 9 days (2012 to 2016)

**Source:** Gereffi et al. (2018); Midea annual reports 2013-2016.

---

### The Key Difference from Ping An

Midea's approach to legacy was fundamentally different from Ping An's:

- **Ping An:** "Surround and strangle." Build the new platform alongside the old; gradually migrate; legacy systems persist but are architecturally marginalized.
- **Midea:** "Standardize, centralize, replace." Impose common processes; consolidate fragmented infrastructure; eliminate redundant systems.

The difference reflects the nature of the legacy landscapes:

- **Ping An's legacy** was diverse (different businesses—insurance, banking, asset management) with regulatory constraints that prevented rapid replacement.
- **Midea's legacy** was fragmented but homogeneous (same business—manufacturing—with inconsistent processes across divisions). Standardization and consolidation were feasible because the underlying business was the same.

## 3. Huawei: Legacy Systems and the IPD/LTC/ITR Transformation

### The Legacy Landscape

By the late 1990s, Huawei had grown rapidly as a telecommunications equipment manufacturer. Its growth had outpaced its management systems:

- **Ad hoc product development:** No standardized development process; each product team worked independently
- **Fragmented engineering assets:** Hardware modules, software libraries, and testing procedures were inconsistent across product lines
- **Inefficient R&D:** Tian Tao (*The Huawei Story*, Chapter 7) reports that approximately 50% of R&D projects failed to produce commercial products before the IPD transformation
- **Disconnected supply chain:** Procurement, manufacturing, and delivery were managed independently with limited integration

**Source:** Tian Tao, *The Huawei Story* (2014), Chapters 6-7; De Cremer et al. (2017), Chapters 7-8.

---

### The Transformation Approach: "Import, Rigidify, Solidify, Optimize"

Huawei's approach to its legacy landscape was the most radical among the documented cases: it imported an entirely new operating system from IBM and imposed it on the existing organization.

**Phase 1: Import and Rigidify (1998-2003)**

- **IBM IPD (Integrated Product Development):** Huawei paid IBM an estimated $2-4 billion over the full engagement. IBM consultants embedded in Huawei's operations.
- **"Rigidify" (僵化):** Ren Zhengfei's documented instruction: for the first two years, Huawei managers were *forbidden* to modify the IBM processes. The legacy way of working was to be replaced, not adapted.
- **Resistance and turnover:** Tian Tao (Chapter 6) reports that several senior Huawei executives resigned in protest. Ren accepted their resignations.

**Phase 2: Solidify (2003-2008)**

- Once the IBM processes were fully embedded, they became the unalterable standard
- Phase-gate reviews enforced compliance
- Shared hardware modules and software libraries were built
- Legacy product development practices were eliminated, not wrapped

**Phase 3: Optimize (2008-present)**

- Only after mastery was achieved was optimization permitted
- Huawei adapted the IPD framework to its specific needs
- Additional process frameworks (LTC: Lead-to-Cash; ITR: Issue-to-Resolution) were developed or imported

**Source:** Tian Tao (2014), Chapters 6-7; De Cremer et al. (2017), Chapter 7.

---

### The Legacy Handling Principle: "Wear American Shoes"

Ren Zhengfei's "wear American shoes" (穿美国鞋) metaphor captures Huawei's legacy approach: the organization must conform to the new system, not the system to the organization. This is the opposite of the "brownfield" adaptation approach common in Western legacy transformation, where new systems are customized to fit existing processes and organizational structures.

**The architectural implication:**

Huawei did not "integrate" its legacy landscape with the new IPD system. It *replaced* the legacy landscape. The cost was enormous (billions in consulting fees, executive turnover, organizational disruption). The benefit was a clean, standardized operating model without the architectural compromises that brownfield integration typically requires.

## 4. Suning: The Legacy Retailer's Failed Transformation

### The Legacy Landscape

Suning was China's largest brick-and-mortar electronics retailer, founded in 1990. By 2013, it operated over 1,600 stores nationwide with a legacy landscape typical of traditional retail:

- **Physical store operations:** Store-based inventory, in-store sales processes, regional management structure
- **Limited online presence:** Suning's e-commerce platform was underdeveloped relative to Alibaba and JD.com
- **Store-centric culture:** Regional managers were evaluated on store sales, not online sales; incentives were aligned with the legacy model
- **No shared platform:** Unlike Alibaba's Middle Platform, Suning had no shared technology infrastructure for online commerce

**Source:** *Caixin*, "Suning's Painful Digital Pivot" (March 2016); Suning annual reports 2013-2019.

---

### The Transformation Approach: "Simultaneous New Build and Legacy Migration"

Suning attempted to build a new e-commerce platform while simultaneously migrating its legacy retail operations to the new model. This is the most difficult transformation approach—and it failed.

**What Suning attempted:**

- **Build a new e-commerce platform:** Suning.com was developed as a new online retail platform
- **Integrate offline and online:** The "One Body, Two Wings" (一体两翼) strategy aimed to create a seamless O2O experience
- **Migrate store operations:** Regional stores were expected to integrate with the online platform
- **Cultural transformation:** Zhang Jindong used the "Old Drunkard" (旧酒鬼) label to stigmatize resistance to the digital pivot

**Source:** *Caixin* (March 2016).

---

### Why It Failed: Documented Factors

*Caixin's* 2016 investigation identified several factors in the transformation's failure:

**1. No shared platform infrastructure**

Unlike Alibaba (which built its Middle Platform before launching new businesses) or Ping An (which built its new platform before migrating legacy businesses), Suning attempted to build the new platform *while* migrating the legacy business *and* competing with Alibaba and JD.com. The new platform was incomplete and unstable; the legacy business was disrupted but not replaced.

**2. Incomplete process standardization**

Unlike Midea (which standardized processes before consolidating infrastructure), Suning attempted to integrate online and offline operations without first standardizing the underlying processes. Store managers were asked to support online orders without clear processes for inventory allocation, order fulfillment, or customer service across channels.

**3. Cultural resistance without structural resolution**

The "Old Drunkard" label created fear but did not provide a viable alternative. Regional managers who had spent decades building store-based businesses were told to embrace e-commerce but were not given the tools, processes, or incentives to do so successfully. The 60% replacement rate of senior regional managers (2013-2015) removed experienced retail operators without replacing them with proven e-commerce leaders.

**4. No strategic investment pool**

Unlike Alibaba (strategic pool), Huawei (strategic reserves), or JD.com (campaign fund), Suning funded its digital transformation through the normal annual budget cycle. E-commerce investments competed with store investments for the same limited capital. The *Caixin* report documents that the online business unit's mid-year funding requests were routinely delayed until the next budget cycle.

**Source:** *Caixin* (March 2016); Suning financial disclosures showing declining profitability 2015-2019.

---

### The Outcome

Suning's legacy transformation failed to achieve sustained competitive success:

- The company remained a significant retailer but lost the e-commerce competition to Alibaba and JD.com
- Profitability declined from 2015 onward
- In 2021, Suning required a state-backed bailout
- The company's market position eroded substantially

**The architectural lesson:**

Suning's failure demonstrates that legacy transformation requires *sequencing*. Ping An built first, migrated later. Midea standardized first, consolidated later. Huawei replaced first, optimized later. Suning attempted to build, migrate, compete, and transform culture simultaneously—and the legacy landscape proved too heavy to lift all at once.

## 5. Cross-Cutting Patterns: How Chinese Firms Handle Legacy

Based on the documented cases, I can identify patterns in how Chinese firms approach legacy landscapes. This is my analytical inference:

### Pattern 1: The "Surround and Strangle" Strategy (Ping An)

- **When used:** Diverse legacy landscape with regulatory or operational constraints that prevent rapid replacement
- **Approach:** Build new platform alongside legacy; prove it with new businesses; gradually migrate legacy processes; wrap remaining legacy systems with APIs
- **Legacy end-state:** Legacy systems persist but are architecturally marginalized—they are back-office record-keepers, not systems of engagement or innovation
- **Risk profile:** Moderate. The new platform is proven before legacy migration begins
- **Transferability:** High. This is the classic "strangler pattern" applied at enterprise scale. European financial services firms with similar legacy constraints can adopt this approach.

### Pattern 2: The "Standardize, Centralize, Replace" Strategy (Midea)

- **When used:** Fragmented but homogeneous legacy landscape (same business, inconsistent processes)
- **Approach:** Impose common processes first; consolidate fragmented infrastructure; eliminate redundant systems; then optimize
- **Legacy end-state:** Legacy systems are eliminated. A single, standardized platform replaces multiple fragmented systems.
- **Risk profile:** Moderate. Process standardization creates organizational resistance, but the underlying business homogeneity makes standardization feasible.
- **Transferability:** High for organizations with fragmented but fundamentally similar business units (e.g., a manufacturer with multiple divisions making similar products).

### Pattern 3: The "Import and Replace" Strategy (Huawei)

- **When used:** Legacy landscape that is fundamentally inadequate for the company's strategic ambitions
- **Approach:** Import a proven external system wholesale; forbid adaptation during the absorption period; enforce compliance rigidly; allow optimization only after mastery
- **Legacy end-state:** Legacy is eliminated. The imported system becomes the new standard.
- **Risk profile:** Very high. Executive turnover, organizational disruption, enormous financial cost. Huawei could pursue this because of Ren Zhengfei's absolute authority and the company's willingness to accept the cost.
- **Transferability:** Conditional. Few organizations have the leadership authority and risk tolerance to impose this approach. The "rigidify-solidify-optimize" sequence is transferable as a principle, but the wholesale import-and-replace model is extreme.

### Pattern 4: The "Simultaneous Build and Migrate" Strategy (Suning) — THE FAILURE PATTERN

- **When attempted:** When a company tries to build a new platform, migrate legacy operations, and compete in the market simultaneously
- **Why it fails:** The legacy landscape consumes resources and management attention needed for the new build; the new platform is unstable because it is being built under competitive pressure; cultural resistance is addressed through exhortation rather than structural change
- **Legacy end-state:** The legacy business is disrupted, the new platform is incomplete, and the company's competitive position deteriorates
- **Transferability:** This is the pattern to avoid. The successful cases (Ping An, Midea, Huawei) all *sequenced* their legacy transformation. None attempted simultaneous build-and-migrate.

## 6. The Architectural Principles for Legacy Transformation

Based on the documented successes and the Suning failure, I can synthesize architectural principles for legacy transformation. These are my analytical inferences:

**Principle 1: Sequence, don't simultaneous-build.** Build the new platform first. Prove it works. Then migrate. Never attempt to build and migrate at the same time under competitive pressure.

**Principle 2: Standardize processes before consolidating technology.** Technology consolidation without process standardization creates automated chaos. Midea standardized T+3 processes before consolidating warehouses and IT systems.

**Principle 3: The new platform must be proven before legacy migration begins.** Ping An launched new businesses (Good Doctor, Lufax) on its new platform before migrating legacy insurance operations. This de-risked the migration by proving the platform worked.

**Principle 4: Legacy systems can be wrapped, not replaced.** Not all legacy systems must be eliminated. Ping An's approach of wrapping legacy systems with APIs and reducing them to back-office record-keeping is often more practical and less risky than full replacement.

**Principle 5: Cultural resistance requires structural resolution, not exhortation.** Zhang Jindong's "Old Drunkard" label created fear but did not provide a viable alternative. Midea's Paul Fang imposed standardized processes and metrics—a structural change that made the new way of working the only way of working. Zhang Ruimin eliminated the middle management layer entirely—a structural change that made the old way of working impossible. Structural change works. Exhortation does not.

**Principle 6: Legacy transformation requires funding outside the normal budget cycle.** Suning's digital initiatives had to compete for annual budget with store operations—and lost. Alibaba, Huawei, and JD.com all used strategic investment pools outside the annual budget cycle to fund transformation. Legacy businesses will always win resource competition against new platforms if both operate within the same budget process.

## 7. The Article Implication

For a European employer audience, the legacy transformation experience of Chinese firms offers both positive and cautionary lessons:

**"Chinese companies inherit complex legacy landscapes just as European companies do. Their advantage is not the absence of legacy but a disciplined approach to transforming it: sequence the transformation (build before migrate), standardize processes before consolidating technology, prove the new platform before migrating the old, and fund transformation outside the normal budget cycle. The Suning failure demonstrates that even in China's high-growth environment, simultaneous build-and-migrate under competitive pressure fails. The principles that worked for Ping An, Midea, and Huawei—sequencing, standardization, proof-before-migration, and separate transformation funding—are directly transferable to a European context."**
