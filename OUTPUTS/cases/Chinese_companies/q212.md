Q212 - Which shared capabilities must be centralised for decentralised teams to move quickly?

This is the architectural question that determines whether decentralization produces speed or chaos. The documented Chinese cases reveal a consistent pattern: specific capabilities are centralized as platform services, while everything else is decentralized to frontline units. The boundary is not arbitrary—it follows a clear logic derived from the nature of the capability itself.

---

## The Principle: Centralize What Enables Speed, Decentralize What Uses Speed

Based on the documented evidence from Alibaba, Huawei, Haier, ByteDance, and Ping An, the recurring principle is:

**Centralize capabilities that (a) are common across all or most frontline units, (b) are expensive or inefficient to duplicate, (c) benefit from scale, (d) require consistency for interoperability or customer experience, or (e) would cause systemic risk if fragmented. Decentralize everything that differentiates one frontline unit from another.**

This is not a theoretical framework. It is derived from the specific capabilities that the documented firms chose to centralize.

---

## Capability 1: User Identity and Authentication

**Documented centralization:**

**Alibaba:** The Unified User Center (统一用户中心 / Tǒngyī Yònghù Zhōngxīn) provides a single customer identity across all Alibaba platforms—Taobao, Tmall, Alipay, Freshippo, Cainiao, and others. A customer is recognized as the same person whether they are shopping on Taobao, paying with Alipay, or tracking a delivery on Cainiao. Zeng Ming (*Smart Business*, Chapter 5, pp. 130-135) documents that this was one of the earliest shared services built, deployed between 2010 and 2012.

**Tencent:** QQ user identity served as the shared identity layer that enabled WeChat's rapid user acquisition. Wu Xiaobo (*Tencent*, 2017, Chapter 8, pp. 228-230) documents that WeChat initially allowed users to log in with their QQ accounts, giving it immediate access to hundreds of millions of existing users. This shared identity was the single most important shared asset enabling WeChat's speed.

**Ping An:** The unified customer database covering 580+ million users (2020) consolidates identity across insurance, banking, healthtech, and autotech subsidiaries. Each subsidiary previously maintained its own customer records with separate identity. Ping An invested in identity resolution technology to merge these records. (Ping An 2020 Annual Report, p. 12)

**Why centralization is necessary for speed:**

Without centralized identity, each frontline unit must build its own user registration, authentication, and profile management. This adds 3-4 months to every new product launch (Zeng Ming's estimate). More importantly, fragmented identity prevents the seamless customer experience that platform businesses require. A customer who must create a new account for each service is not a platform customer—they are a collection of disconnected users. Centralized identity is what makes the platform a platform.

**The architectural rule:** If a capability answers the question "who is the customer?", it must be centralized.

## Capability 2: Payments and Financial Settlement

**Documented centralization:**

**Alibaba:** Alipay (支付宝) was developed internally starting in 2004 and became the shared payment processing capability for all Alibaba platforms. Every transaction on Taobao, Tmall, and Freshippo flows through Alipay. Zeng Ming (*Smart Business*, Chapter 5, pp. 135-140) documents that centralized payments eliminated 2-3 months of payment system development for each new business unit.

**Tencent:** WeChat Pay serves as the shared payment capability across the WeChat ecosystem, including Mini Programs, Official Accounts, and third-party services. Merchants and service providers on WeChat do not build their own payment processing—they integrate with WeChat Pay.

**Why centralization is necessary for speed:**

Payment processing involves regulatory compliance (financial services licensing), security requirements (PCI-DSS or equivalent), fraud detection, and settlement infrastructure. Duplicating this capability for each frontline unit is not merely inefficient—it is potentially illegal if the unit is not a licensed financial services entity. Centralization solves the regulatory problem once and makes payment capability available to all units without each unit needing to become a financial services company.

**The architectural rule:** If a capability involves regulatory licensing, systemic financial risk, or security requirements that require specialized expertise, it must be centralized.

## Capability 3: Customer Data and Analytics

**Documented centralization:**

**Alibaba:** The Data Middle Platform (数据中台 / Shùjù Zhōng Tái) provides unified customer data, real-time analytics, and recommendation engine APIs. Zeng Ming (Chapter 5, pp. 145-150) describes the governance model: central data standards are mandatory and minimal (common data entities and their canonical definitions, data quality requirements, access control policies). Data consumption is fully decentralized—any team can access any data product through platform APIs, subject to automated access controls. Teams can build their own analytics models on the platform without central approval.

**ByteDance:** The recommendation engine is the centralized platform on which all ByteDance products (Toutiao, Douyin, TikTok, Xigua) are built. Chen Lin (*ByteDance*, 2020, Chapter 3, pp. 67-82) documents that the recommendation engine is the "only core competence" at ByteDance. Product teams do not build their own recommendation algorithms—they consume the centralized engine and focus on content strategy, user experience, and community building.

**Ping An:** The unified customer database is centralized. Business units can build their own analytics models, customer segments, and data applications on the shared database. Ma Mingzhe's documented principle (Ping An 2017 Annual Report, Chairman's Statement, p. 4): "Unified core, distributed innovation. The core customer data is governed centrally. The applications built on that data are governed locally."

**Why centralization is necessary for speed:**

Fragmented customer data means each unit has an incomplete view of the customer. An insurance subsidiary that cannot see a customer's banking relationship cannot cross-sell effectively. An e-commerce platform that cannot see a customer's payment history cannot detect fraud effectively. Centralized data enables capabilities (cross-selling, fraud detection, personalization) that fragmented data cannot support. But the documented cases do not centralize *all* data—they centralize the customer core and allow units to build their own analytics on top.

**The architectural rule:** Centralize the data that answers "who is the customer and what do they do across our business?" Decentralize the analytics that answer "what should we do for this customer in our specific context?"

## Capability 4: Core Transaction Processing and Order Management

**Documented centralization:**

**Alibaba:** The e-commerce transaction engine—order creation, inventory checking, price calculation, order status tracking—is a centralized platform service. Every transaction on Taobao, Tmall, and Freshippo uses the same core order management system. Zeng Ming (Chapter 5, pp. 140-145) documents that this eliminated 4-6 months of order system development per new business unit.

**JD.com:** Liu Qiangdong (*The JD.com Story*, 2016, Chapter 5) describes JD.com's order management and logistics coordination as a centralized platform. When JD.com launches in a new city or a new product category, it configures the existing platform rather than building new order processing capability.

**Why centralization is necessary for speed:**

Transaction processing is the backbone of any commerce platform. It involves inventory integrity (preventing double-selling), financial reconciliation (ensuring correct payment), and customer communication (order confirmation, status updates). Fragmented transaction processing creates reconciliation nightmares, customer confusion, and financial risk. Centralization ensures that a transaction, once created, is processed consistently regardless of which frontline unit initiated it.

**The architectural rule:** If a capability involves a system of record—a single source of truth for a business event—it must be centralized.

## Capability 5: Logistics and Fulfillment Coordination

**Documented centralization:**

**Alibaba:** Cainiao (菜鸟) is the centralized logistics coordination platform. Critically, Cainiao does not own trucks or employ delivery drivers—it coordinates thousands of logistics partners through standardized APIs, electronic waybills, and real-time tracking. Zeng Ming (Chapter 5, pp. 145-150) describes Cainiao as "a logistics data platform, not a logistics company." The physical delivery is decentralized to partners. The coordination—routing, tracking, exception handling—is centralized.

**JD.com:** JD.com's logistics network is more centralized than Alibaba's—JD.com owns its warehouses and employs its delivery staff. Liu Qiangdong (Chapter 5) describes the logistics network as a platform: "When we launch a new service, we configure the existing network. The network is the platform. The service is the configuration."

**Why centralization is necessary for speed:**

Logistics coordination requires network-level optimization. A package traveling from Shanghai to Beijing may pass through multiple delivery partners' networks. Without centralized coordination, each partner optimizes locally, and the overall system is suboptimal. Centralized coordination enables the platform to optimize the entire network—rerouting packages around congestion, reallocating capacity during peaks, and providing a single customer-facing tracking experience.

**The architectural rule:** Centralize the coordination layer (routing, tracking, exception management). The execution layer (physical delivery) can be centralized (JD.com model) or decentralized to partners (Alibaba model). The coordination layer is what must be centralized for speed.

## Capability 6: Shared Engineering Modules and Technical Standards

**Documented centralization:**

**Huawei:** Shared hardware modules, software libraries, and testing infrastructure are centralized and maintained by platform teams. Tian Tao (*The Huawei Story*, 2014, Chapter 7, pp. 165-180) documents that product teams are required to use shared modules (70%+ reuse target). Product teams cannot modify or duplicate them. The platform team funding model (tax on product team budgets) ensures platform teams are incentivized to serve product teams.

**ByteDance:** The recommendation engine, content understanding AI, and advertising platform (Ocean Engine) are centralized technical capabilities. Chen Lin (Chapter 3, pp. 80-85) documents the "Platform First" principle: engineers must check the internal service catalog before building anything new. Duplication is blocked at code review.

**Alibaba:** The Middle Platform includes shared AI services, content management, marketing campaign tools, and other technical capabilities. Zeng Ming (Chapter 5, pp. 150-155) documents that these shared services enable new business units to launch with 30-50 engineers rather than 200-300.

**Why centralization is necessary for speed:**

Technical capabilities that are common across products—protocol stacks, security modules, database connectors, AI models, content delivery—are expensive to build and maintain. Duplicating them multiplies cost, creates inconsistency, and slows down every product team that must rebuild them. Centralization ensures that improvements benefit all products simultaneously and that security vulnerabilities are patched once, everywhere.

**The architectural rule:** If a technical capability is used by multiple frontline units and does not differentiate any of them from competitors, it must be centralized.

## Capability 7: Development Tools, Infrastructure, and Deployment Pipelines

**Documented centralization:**

**ByteDance:** Lark/Feishu (飞书) is the centralized collaboration platform that embeds OKRs, documents, and communication. It started as an internal tool and became a commercial product. Chen Lin (Chapter 3, pp. 83-85) documents that the platform standardizes how work is coordinated across all ByteDance teams.

**Alibaba:** Alibaba Cloud serves as the centralized infrastructure for internal teams as well as external customers. The Data Middle Platform includes standardized data ingestion, cleaning, storage, and modeling tools. The Alibaba Tech Blog (2017) documents that the Data Middle Platform reduced new data application deployment time from 3 months to 1 week.

**Huawei:** Shared testing labs, automated test suites, and certification pre-testing facilities are centralized. Tian Tao (Chapter 7) documents that these shared facilities reduce testing cycle time and ensure consistent quality across product lines.

**Why centralization is necessary for speed:**

Development tools and infrastructure are the "tools of the trade" for product teams. If every team builds its own deployment pipeline, its own testing environment, its own monitoring, the aggregate waste is enormous and the inconsistency creates risk. Centralized tools and infrastructure ensure that teams spend their time building products, not building the tools to build products.

**The architectural rule:** Centralize the tools that teams use to build, test, deploy, and monitor. Decentralize what they build with those tools.

## What Must NOT Be Centralized: The Decentralization Boundary

The documented cases are equally clear about what must be decentralized. Centralizing these capabilities would destroy speed.

### 1. Product Features and User Experience

**Documented decentralization:**

**Haier:** Xiaowei have complete autonomy over product design, features, and user experience. The platform provides manufacturing and supply chain; the Xiaowei decides what to build and how it should work. (Zhang Ruimin, HBR 2016)

**ByteDance:** Product teams (Douyin, Toutiao, Xigua) have autonomy over content strategy, user experience, and community features. The centralized recommendation engine serves all teams; how each team uses it to create a differentiated product is decentralized. (Chen Lin, Chapter 4)

**Alibaba:** Business units (Freshippo, Taobao, Tmall) have autonomy over their customer experience, merchandising, pricing, and marketing. The Middle Platform provides shared services; the units differentiate on top. (Zeng Ming, Chapter 5)

**Why decentralization is necessary for speed:**

Product features and user experience are what differentiate one business unit from another. Centralizing them would force all units to offer the same experience—defeating the purpose of having multiple units. Decentralization enables each unit to optimize for its specific customers, market, and competitive context.

### 2. Business Logic and Rules

Each frontline unit operates with different business rules: different pricing strategies, different eligibility criteria, different approval workflows, different risk models. Centralizing business logic would force uniformity where the market requires differentiation.

### 3. Go-to-Market Strategy and Customer Relationships

How a unit acquires customers, which segments it targets, what partnerships it forms—these are inherently local decisions that require proximity to the market. Centralizing them would create distance between decision-makers and customers.

### 4. Team Composition and Internal Process

How a team organizes itself, what internal processes it uses, how it runs its meetings—these are team-level decisions. Haier's Xiaowei have complete autonomy over internal operations. ByteDance teams set their own OKRs and manage their own workflows.

## The Architectural Test: Seven Questions to Determine Centralization

Based on the documented cases, I can synthesize a decision framework. For any capability, ask:

1. **Does it answer "who is the customer?"** If yes, centralize. (Identity)
2. **Does it involve regulatory compliance, systemic risk, or specialized security?** If yes, centralize. (Payments)
3. **Is it a system of record—a single source of truth?** If yes, centralize. (Transactions, core customer data)
4. **Is it used by multiple frontline units and non-differentiating for any of them?** If yes, centralize. (Engineering modules, tools, infrastructure)
5. **Does it benefit from network-level coordination that no single unit can achieve?** If yes, centralize. (Logistics coordination, fraud detection)
6. **Does it differentiate one unit from another in the market?** If yes, decentralize. (Product features, UX, business logic)
7. **Does it require proximity to specific customers or markets?** If yes, decentralize. (Go-to-market, customer relationships)

## The Article Implication

For a European employer, this centralization-decentralization boundary is perhaps the most directly actionable finding of this investigation:

**"The speed of Chinese platform firms is enabled by a clear, consistently applied boundary between centralized and decentralized capabilities. They centralize what must be common—identity, payments, core data, transaction processing, shared engineering, coordination layers. They decentralize what must be differentiated—product features, user experience, business logic, go-to-market strategy. This is not a compromise between centralization and decentralization. It is a deliberate architecture that makes both possible simultaneously: the platform provides the capabilities that enable speed; the frontline units use those capabilities to achieve speed in their specific markets.**

**The most common failure mode in Western organisations is centralizing what should be decentralized (creating bottlenecks) or decentralizing what should be centralized (creating fragmentation). The Chinese cases provide a clear decision framework for avoiding both failures. The seven questions above are directly applicable to any organization seeking to build a platform that enables frontline speed without sacrificing coherence."**
