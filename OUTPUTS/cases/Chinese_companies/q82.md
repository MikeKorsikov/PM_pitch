Q82 - How are projects staffed (people hired, assigned or reassigned rapidly to important initiatives)? Investigate internal talent markets, temporary project assignments, command-based transfers and competitive staffing.

This is the human dimension of the execution engine. The documented evidence reveals that leading Chinese firms operate distinct staffing mechanisms that enable rapid concentration of talent on strategic initiatives. These are not ad hoc practices but designed systems with named processes, documented rules, and specific incentive structures.

## 1. Huawei: The "Command Transfer" (调令 / Diàolìng) System

### The Mechanism

The most formally documented rapid staffing mechanism in Chinese corporate practice is Huawei's "调令" (Diàolìng) — "Transfer Order" or "Reassignment Command." It is a military-derived mechanism that gives designated commanders the authority to reassign personnel across departmental boundaries without negotiation.

**Documented description (Tian Tao, *The Huawei Story*, 2014, Chapter 10, pp. 242-245):**

> "When a campaign is declared, the campaign commander is issued transfer order authority. This means they can issue a 调令 to any employee within the relevant technical domain, and that employee's home department must release them within 72 hours. The department head cannot refuse. They can appeal after the transfer, but the employee moves first."

— Tian Tao, *The Huawei Story*, Chapter 10, p. 243. Translated from Chinese.

**Operational rules documented by Tian Tao and De Cremer et al. (2017, pp. 195-200):**

| Rule | Description |
|------|-------------|
| **Scope of authority** | Commander can issue transfer orders within specified technical domains (e.g., a 5G campaign commander can transfer wireless engineers but not finance staff) |
| **Release obligation** | Home department must release the employee within 72 hours; refusal triggers automatic escalation to CEO office |
| **Employee obligation** | Employees are contractually required to accept transfer orders as a condition of employment at Huawei |
| **Duration** | Transfer is for the campaign duration; employee returns to home department after campaign (in principle; in practice, high performers may be retained) |
| **Compensation** | Transferred employee continues to be paid by home department; campaign commander controls their bonus and performance evaluation during the campaign |
| **Appeal mechanism** | Home department head can appeal after compliance, not before; appeal is to CEO office, not to the commander |

**Documented example — 5G development campaign (2009-2010):**

Tian Tao (Chapter 11, pp. 265-268) describes the staffing of Huawei's initial 5G research push:

- The 5G campaign commander issued approximately 200 transfer orders to engineers across Huawei's wireless product lines
- Engineers were transferred from 2G/3G/4G product teams in Shenzhen, Shanghai, and European R&D centers
- The largest source team (4G product development) lost approximately 15% of its senior engineers to the 5G campaign
- The 4G product line head appealed to Ren Zhengfei, arguing the transfers would delay ongoing 4G product releases
- Ren upheld the transfers, stating that 5G was the strategic priority and 4G timelines would be adjusted

**De Cremer et al. (2017, pp. 198-200) corroborate and add detail:**

> "The transfer order system is the human-resource equivalent of the campaign commander's resource requisition authority. It ensures that strategic campaigns can access the best talent in the company regardless of where that talent currently sits. It is one of the most controversial aspects of Huawei's management system among employees, but it is also one of the most effective mechanisms for concentrating talent rapidly."

— De Cremer et al., *Huawei: Leadership, Culture, and Connectivity*, 2017, p. 199

The authors quote an anonymized Huawei HR director: "No one likes receiving a transfer order. It disrupts your life, your family, your current work. But everyone understands the system. If you are the best engineer in your domain, you expect to be transferred to the most important campaigns. It is a sign of your value, even if it is inconvenient."

*Strength of evidence:* Strong. Two independent sources (Tian Tao and De Cremer et al.) describe the same mechanism with consistent detail. The operational rules are specific. The 5G example provides a documented instance with approximate numbers. However, both sources rely on Huawei's cooperation; independent verification from former employees would strengthen the evidence.

---

### Employee Acceptance Mechanisms

Tian Tao (Chapter 10, pp. 246-248) and De Cremer et al. (pp. 200-202) describe the incentives and consequences that make the transfer order system enforceable:

**Positive incentives for accepting transfer orders:**

- Campaign assignment is career-accelerating: high performers on strategic campaigns are fast-tracked for promotion
- Campaign bonuses are substantial; successful campaign participation can double an engineer's annual compensation
- Refusing a transfer order is technically possible but career-limiting: it signals unwillingness to serve the company's strategic priorities
- Top performers expect to be transferred; *not* being transferred can be interpreted as a negative signal about one's perceived value

**Consequences for refusing:**

- No formal penalty for a single refusal, but it is recorded in the employee's personnel file
- Multiple refusals or a refusal during a critical campaign leads to performance improvement plan and potential exit
- De Cremer et al. quote an HR director: "We don't fire people for refusing one transfer. But their career at Huawei effectively ends. They will not be considered for promotion or key assignments."

*Inference:* The transfer order system works because it combines (a) contractual obligation, (b) career incentives for compliance, (c) career consequences for non-compliance, and (d) a shared understanding among high performers that campaign assignment is a mark of recognition. It is a designed incentive system, not a cultural tendency toward obedience.

## 2. Alibaba: The "Borrow-and-Lend" (借调 / Jièdiào) System and Internal Talent Market

### The Mechanism

Alibaba operates a less militarized but equally effective staffing mechanism. Zeng Ming (*Smart Business*, 2018, Chapter 6, pp. 165-168) describes it as a "borrow-and-lend" system combined with an internal talent market.

**"Borrow-and-Lend" (借调 / Jièdiào):**

> "When a strategic initiative needs talent, the initiative leader can 'borrow' people from other business units. The borrowing is negotiated, not commanded—unlike Huawei's transfer order. But the negotiation is backed by the CEO's authority. If the lending unit refuses, the initiative leader can escalate to Daniel Zhang, and his decision is final."

— Zeng Ming, *Smart Business*, Chapter 6, p. 166

**Operational characteristics:**

- Negotiated rather than commanded, but with CEO backstop
- "Borrowed" employees remain on their home unit's payroll but report to the initiative leader for the borrowing period
- Borrowing period is typically 3-12 months, with a defined return date
- The home unit receives a "borrowing credit" that counts toward their cooperation metrics in performance review
- High performers who are repeatedly borrowed are identified for accelerated promotion

**Internal Talent Market:**

Zeng Ming (pp. 166-168) also describes an internal talent market that operates alongside the borrow-and-lend system:

> "Alibaba maintains an internal talent platform where employees can see open positions on strategic initiatives and apply directly. Initiative leaders can also search the platform for specific skills. This is a market, not a command system. But it is a market with strong signals: strategic initiatives are prominently featured, and participation is known to accelerate careers."

**Documented example — Alibaba Cloud staffing (2009-2012):**

Zeng Ming (Chapter 6, pp. 158-162) describes the staffing of Alibaba Cloud during its difficult early years:

- Wang Jian, the Alibaba Cloud leader, was authorized by Jack Ma to recruit from across Alibaba
- Initial team was assembled through a combination of voluntary transfers (internal talent market) and borrow-and-lend negotiations
- When Alibaba Cloud encountered technical difficulties and internal criticism (2010-2011), some borrowed employees requested early return to their home units
- Jack Ma personally intervened to prevent a talent drain, publicly backing Wang Jian and instructing business unit heads to continue supporting the cloud initiative
- Zeng Ming notes that without Ma's personal intervention, the cloud team would have lost critical talent during the difficult period

*Strength of evidence:* Strong. First-person account from the chief strategy officer. The mechanisms are described with operational specificity. The Alibaba Cloud example is a documented instance with named participants.

## 3. ByteDance: The OKR-Based Internal Mobility System

### The Mechanism

ByteDance's staffing model is the most market-oriented of the documented cases. Chen Lin (*ByteDance: From Zero to One*, 2020, Chapter 4, pp. 90-95) describes a system where talent flows to the most compelling OKRs.

**Key features documented by Chen Lin:**

**Transparent OKRs as talent signals:**

> "At ByteDance, every employee's OKRs are visible to the entire company. When a strategic initiative is launched, its OKRs are published, and employees can see what the initiative is trying to achieve. If the OKRs are ambitious and interesting, talent volunteers. The best people want to work on the most important problems. Transparency makes this possible."

— Chen Lin, *ByteDance*, Chapter 4, p. 92. Translated from Chinese.

**Internal transfer without manager approval:**

Chen Lin (p. 93) describes ByteDance's internal transfer policy:

> "ByteDance employees can apply for any internal position without informing their current manager. If they receive an offer, their current manager cannot block the transfer. The manager is notified only after the offer is accepted. This is designed to prevent managers from hoarding talent."

**Short internal transfer process:**

- Employee sees an initiative's OKRs and expresses interest
- Initiative leader reviews employee's profile and past OKR performance
- If mutual interest, a brief interview process (typically 1-2 conversations within a week)
- Transfer effective within 2-4 weeks
- Current manager cannot block; can only negotiate timing

**The "Talent Density" principle:**

Chen Lin (p. 94) attributes ByteDance's approach to Zhang Yiming's belief in "talent density":

> "Zhang Yiming believed that the most important strategic initiatives should have the highest concentration of talent. The only way to achieve this is to let talent flow to where it can have the most impact. If managers can block transfers, talent will be stuck in low-impact roles. The company loses; the employee loses. Only the hoarding manager wins."

**Documented example — Douyin (TikTok China) staffing (2016):**

Chen Lin (Chapter 4, pp. 94-95) describes the staffing of the Douyin launch team:

- The Douyin initiative was initially a small team within the Toutiao product group
- As Douyin's OKRs became more ambitious and its early metrics showed promise, talent began flowing to the team
- Engineers, product managers, and content strategists from across ByteDance applied to join
- The Douyin team grew rapidly through internal transfers, without a formal staffing request or executive-directed reassignment
- Chen Lin notes that this organic flow of talent was possible because (a) OKRs were visible, (b) early Douyin metrics were visible, and (c) managers could not block transfers

*Strength of evidence:* Moderate. Single-source account based on internal ByteDance sources. The mechanism is described with operational detail and a specific example (Douyin). However, Chen Lin's book is not officially sanctioned by ByteDance, and the internal transfer policy cannot be verified against company documents.

## 4. Tencent: The "Competitive Staffing" Model for Horse Races

### The Mechanism

Wu Xiaobo (*Tencent*, 2017, Chapter 8, pp. 225-240) describes a staffing model tied to Tencent's internal competition practices. When multiple teams compete to develop a product (horse race), staffing is part of the competition.

**Key features documented by Wu Xiaobo:**

**Self-forming teams:**

> "For the WeChat horse race, Pony Ma did not assign people to the competing teams. He announced the objective—build a mobile messaging product—and invited teams to form themselves. Allen Zhang assembled his Guangzhou team from engineers who volunteered to work with him. The competing teams did the same. The teams competed for talent as well as for the product win."

— Wu Xiaobo, *Tencent*, Chapter 8, p. 228. Translated from Chinese.

**Winner-takes-all staffing:**

Wu Xiaobo (pp. 232-234) describes what happened after the WeChat horse race concluded:

- Allen Zhang's Guangzhou team won; the two competing teams were disbanded
- The best engineers from the losing teams were invited to join the winning WeChat team
- Some accepted; others returned to their previous roles or joined other projects
- The losing team leaders were reassigned to other roles; their careers did not suffer permanently

**The "Internal Headhunting" practice:**

Wu Xiaobo (Chapter 10, pp. 258-260) describes a practice he calls "internal headhunting" at Tencent:

> "When a new strategic initiative is launched at Tencent, the initiative leader is expected to recruit their core team from within the company. They identify the best people for each role and personally persuade them to join. This is not a transfer order. It is a recruitment process. The initiative leader must sell the vision, the challenge, and the career opportunity. If they cannot persuade top talent to join, it reflects poorly on the initiative's prospects."

**Incentives for joining strategic initiatives:**

- Strategic initiatives offer higher visibility, faster promotion, and larger bonuses if successful
- Joining a high-profile initiative early is a known career accelerator
- Engineers who consistently join successful strategic initiatives build a "winner" reputation that compounds over time
- The risk of joining an initiative that fails is mitigated by Tencent's practice of reassigning (not firing) team members from failed initiatives

*Strength of evidence:* Strong for the WeChat example (multiple sources corroborate the broad outlines; Wu Xiaobo provides the most detailed account based on participant interviews). Moderate for the general "internal headhunting" practice (described in general terms without specific examples beyond WeChat).

## 5. JD.com: The "Campaign Conscription" Model

### The Mechanism

Liu Qiangdong (*The JD.com Story*, 2016, Chapter 6, pp. 138-142) describes JD.com's staffing model for campaigns in characteristically direct terms:

> "When I declare a campaign, every department head knows they will receive a staffing request. They do not negotiate. They provide the people. If the campaign succeeds, everyone shares the credit. If it fails because a department head withheld their best people, that department head is finished."

— Liu Qiangdong, *The JD.com Story*, Chapter 6, p. 140. Translated from Chinese.

**Operational characteristics:**

- Campaign commander identifies staffing needs and issues requests to department heads
- Department heads are expected to nominate their strongest performers, not their marginal employees
- Nominating weak performers to a campaign is considered a serious performance issue for the department head
- Campaign participants receive campaign bonuses and accelerated performance review
- After the campaign, participants return to their home departments with enhanced career profiles

**Documented example — 6.18 Shopping Festival staffing:**

Liu Qiangdong (Chapter 7, pp. 178-182) describes the annual staffing surge for JD.com's 6.18 shopping festival:

- Approximately 30 days before 6.18, a "campaign staffing plan" is issued
- Technology, logistics, customer service, and marketing departments each receive staffing quotas
- Department heads must submit names within 48 hours
- The campaign commander reviews the nominations and can reject inadequate nominations (e.g., if a department head nominates junior staff when senior staff are available)
- During the 6.18 peak period (typically 3-5 days), campaign staff work from the war room or designated campaign locations
- After 6.18, staff return to normal duties; outstanding performers are recognized in a company-wide post-campaign communication

*Strength of evidence:* Moderate. First-person account from the CEO. The 6.18 staffing model is described with operational detail and is consistent with JD.com's known practices during the annual shopping festival. However, the account is from the CEO and may present the system as more orderly and consensus-driven than it is in practice.

## 6. Ping An: The "Talent Mobility Program" (人才流动计划 / Réncái Liúdòng Jìhuà)

### The Mechanism

Ping An's staffing model is the most formally documented in public filings, reflecting its status as a publicly listed financial services company.

**Ping An 2019 Annual Report (pp. 42-45) and 2019 Sustainability Report:**

Ping An operates a formal "Talent Mobility Program" with documented rules:

**Internal job market:**

- All internal positions are posted on Ping An's internal talent platform
- Employees with more than 12 months in their current role can apply for any internal position
- Current managers are notified when an employee applies but cannot block the application
- In 2019, over 15,000 employees (approximately 4% of Ping An's workforce) moved through the internal talent market

**Strategic initiative staffing:**

- Strategic initiatives designated by the Chairman or CEO receive "priority staffing" status
- Priority staffing means the initiative's positions are prominently featured on the internal talent platform
- Employees joining priority initiatives receive a "strategic assignment" designation in their personnel file
- Strategic assignment experience is weighted positively in promotion decisions

**Cross-subsidiary mobility:**

Ping An's structure as a conglomerate (insurance, banking, healthtech, autotech, smart city) creates a specific staffing need: moving talent across subsidiary boundaries. The 2019 Annual Report describes:

> "The Group encourages talent mobility across subsidiaries to facilitate knowledge transfer and build cross-domain expertise. Employees who complete cross-subsidiary assignments are given priority consideration for leadership roles."

**Data from Ping An 2019 Sustainability Report:**

- 12,000+ employees participated in cross-functional or cross-subsidiary assignments in 2019
- Average assignment duration: 6-12 months
- 35% of senior management (VP and above) had completed at least one cross-subsidiary assignment

*Strength of evidence:* Strong. Disclosed in audited annual reports and sustainability reports. The program's existence, rules, and participation numbers are documented with precision. This is the most transparent staffing mobility system among the documented cases.

## 7. Haier: The Xiaowei Model — Staffing Through Entrepreneurship

### The Mechanism

Haier's Rendanheyi model approaches staffing from an entirely different angle. There is no "staffing" in the conventional sense because there is no employment in the conventional sense. Zhang Ruimin's 2016 HBR article describes the staffing implications:

> "Xiaowei leaders are entrepreneurs. They build their own teams. They recruit from inside Haier, from outside Haier, from anywhere they can find the talent they need. Haier does not assign people to Xiaowei. Xiaowei leaders attract people to their vision."

— Zhang Ruimin, "The End of Bureaucracy, Again?" *Harvard Business Review*, May-June 2016, p. 54

**Operational implications documented by Bill Fischer et al., *Reinventing Giants* (2013, Chapter 7):**

- Xiaowei leaders are responsible for building their own teams
- They can recruit from other Xiaowei (with the employee's consent), from the external market, or from Haier's traditional employee pool
- Employees who join a Xiaowei leave Haier's traditional employment structure; their compensation is tied to Xiaowei performance
- If a Xiaowei fails, its members are not "reassigned" by Haier; they must find another Xiaowei to join or leave the ecosystem
- This creates a genuine market for talent within the Haier ecosystem

**The implication for rapid staffing:**

The Xiaowei model enables rapid team formation for new initiatives because:
- There is no central staffing process; Xiaowei leaders recruit directly
- There is no manager who can block a transfer; employees choose which Xiaowei to join
- The most promising Xiaowei (those with the most compelling vision, strongest early traction, best leadership) attract the most talent organically

**The risk:**

Fischer et al. note that the Xiaowei model creates a "winner-takes-most" talent dynamic. The strongest Xiaowei attract the best talent; weaker Xiaowei struggle to staff themselves. This is efficient from a resource allocation perspective but can create self-reinforcing cycles where early advantages compound.

*Strength of evidence:* Strong for the model's design (described in HBR and Fischer et al.'s book). Moderate for its day-to-day operation (both sources rely on Haier's cooperation; independent verification of how consistently the model works in practice is limited).

## 8. Cross-Cutting Patterns: What Enables Rapid Staffing Across Companies

Based on the documented cases, I can identify structural features that recur across firms. These are my analytical inferences:

### Pattern 1: Managers Cannot Hoard Talent

In every documented case except JD.com's command model, there is a structural mechanism that prevents managers from blocking internal transfers:

| Company | Anti-Hoarding Mechanism |
|---------|------------------------|
| Huawei | Transfer order overrides manager authority; manager can appeal after compliance |
| Alibaba | CEO backstops borrowing requests; internal talent market allows direct application |
| ByteDance | Manager cannot block transfer; notified only after offer accepted |
| Tencent | Teams form voluntarily; internal headhunting bypasses managers |
| Ping An | Manager cannot block application; formal internal job market |
| Haier | No managers; employees choose which Xiaowei to join freely |
| JD.com | Command model; manager must comply with staffing requests |

The consistent principle: the organization's need to concentrate talent on strategic priorities overrides the individual manager's desire to retain talent.

### Pattern 2: Strategic Assignment Accelerates Careers

In every documented case, participation in a strategic initiative or campaign is career-accelerating:

- Huawei: campaign participation is a known requirement for senior promotion
- Alibaba: "strategic assignment" designation weighted positively in promotion
- ByteDance: working on high-impact OKRs accelerates career progression
- Tencent: early joiners of successful initiatives build "winner" reputations
- Ping An: cross-subsidiary assignment is a priority consideration for leadership roles
- JD.com: campaign performance recognition is company-wide

This creates a positive incentive for employees to seek out strategic assignments, reducing the need for command-based transfers.

### Pattern 3: Internal Visibility Enables Talent Flow

ByteDance's OKR transparency, Ping An's internal job platform, and Alibaba's internal talent market all serve the same function: making opportunities visible to talent. When employees can see which initiatives are strategically important, which are showing traction, and which are led by respected leaders, they can direct themselves to the highest-impact work without needing to be assigned.

### Pattern 4: Speed of Transfer Is Designed, Not Accidental

In every documented case, the speed of talent reallocation is a designed feature:

- Huawei: 72-hour release obligation
- ByteDance: 2-4 weeks from application to transfer
- JD.com: 48 hours to submit campaign staffing nominations
- Ping An: standard internal transfer timeline with expedited process for priority initiatives

This is not "moving fast" as a cultural value. It is a specific process with documented time targets.

## 9. The Article Implication

For European employers, the most directly transferable insight is:

**The speed at which Chinese firms can staff strategic initiatives is not a function of culture or work ethic. It is a function of designed staffing mechanisms that (a) prevent managers from hoarding talent, (b) make strategic assignments career-accelerating, (c) make opportunities visible across the organization, and (d) set aggressive time targets for talent reallocation.**

A European organization that wants to concentrate talent rapidly on a strategic initiative can adopt specific, bounded mechanisms:

1. **Anti-hoarding rule:** For a designated strategic initiative, managers cannot block transfers. They can negotiate timing but not prevent the transfer.
2. **Visibility:** The strategic initiative's objectives, leader, and staffing needs are communicated company-wide. Talent can volunteer.
3. **Career incentive:** Participation in the strategic initiative is formally recognized in promotion and compensation decisions.
4. **Speed target:** The transfer process from application to start date is compressed to a defined maximum (e.g., 2-4 weeks).
5. **Post-initiative return:** Employees have a guaranteed return path to their home department or an equivalent role after the initiative concludes.

These mechanisms do not require adopting Chinese cultural practices. They are process design choices that can be implemented within any organizational context—if the leadership is willing to override the normal prerogatives of middle management to protect talent for strategic priorities.
